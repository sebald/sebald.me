# Pushback: Game Spec

Oct 26, 2026 · @Sebastian · revised Oct 27

## Overview

Pushback is a small browser game on sebald.me: a duel of phrases and counters between the roles of a product team, in the pixel-art style of the note headers. The player picks a role (PM, Dev or Designer), works through a gauntlet of colleagues and faces the Stakeholder as the final boss. Each exchange is won by picking the right counter to the phrase just thrown, or by throwing a phrase the opponent cannot answer; the duel is won by filling your buy-in bar first. The player starts knowing their own role's lines and picks up everyone else's in the room.

The three rules on the title screen: Counter the phrase. Fill your buy-in. Full momentum lets you drop the mic.

Scope for v1:

- One route, `/lab/pushback`, no backend, no accounts; progress lives in `localStorage`.
- Three playable roles, a cast of nine colleagues (three per role) of which each run draws six, one boss. A run is seven duels, 12 to 15 minutes.
- 78 lines of dialogue (24 phrase/counter pairs, 6 boss phrases, 24 barks and fallbacks), all original text. The mechanic is borrowed from the Monkey Island insult sword fighting, the words are not. The collecting is kept in a form that fits a mini game: the player's own pool is known from the start, the other two are collected by hearing them, and a lost exchange is never a dead end. The two bars are borrowed from fighting games and that is where the borrowing stops.
- Four backgrounds made with the existing `pixel-header` pipeline, character sprites generated by Gemini at their native pixel size.
- Keyboard and touch playable, 480 by 270 stage scaled by integers to the viewport.
- A result card after the boss that can be copied as text.

Out of scope for v1: sound (the repo has an audio folder, but music is a v2 question), a map or overworld, multiplayer, localisation (English only, like the blog), and any analytics beyond the existing Vercel counter. Role passives and a daily seed are listed under After v1.

Tone: dry and affectionate. Every role gets roasted and every role gets to roast back. Nobody is the villain, not even the Stakeholder, who is merely very busy.

## Core mechanic

A duel is a sequence of exchanges. The attacker throws a phrase, the defender picks a counter from a short menu; the right counter wins the exchange and swaps the initiative, a miss keeps it with the attacker. Every won exchange fills one segment of the winner's buy-in bar. Full buy-in wins the duel.

### The two bars

Each duellist has two bars above their head, drawn as native-size chrome:

- **Buy-in**, three segments (four against the boss). One segment per won exchange, two for a landed mic drop. Full bar wins. It only fills; nothing drains. This is the score, not health: both players build towards something.
- **Momentum**, three steps. Playing well fills it, losing an exchange empties it. Full momentum unlocks the mic drop.

| Event                                                         | Momentum                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Counter before the silence bar is half full                   | +1                                  |
| Counter after that (or with the silence bar off: any counter) | +1 only when the bar is off, else 0 |
| Landed phrase (the opponent missed)                           | +1                                  |
| Lost exchange (missed counter or a countered phrase)          | reset to 0                          |

### The mic drop

With full momentum and the initiative, the attacker can drop the mic: the next phrase is worth two buy-in segments instead of one. It costs the whole momentum bar and raises the stakes for the defender: the counter menu grows to eight and the silence bar runs at half time. The opponent can drop the mic too, telegraphed by the taunt pose and a `Mic drop!` label over the dialogue box, so a full enemy momentum bar is a visible threat.

For the AI as defender the menu size makes no difference, so a player's mic drop against it is simply worth two; the decision it leaves the player is which phrase to spend it on (see Who knows what: a mic drop with a phrase from the opponent's own pool is a wasted bar). Momentum resets on any lost exchange, so holding a full bar for the perfect moment is the risk and spending it now is the safe play.

### The exchange, step by step

1. Attacker steps forward, line types out at 30 characters per second (tap or Space to complete instantly).
2. Defender gets a menu of counters in random order: the one that answers the phrase plus decoys. Menu size and decoy source depend on the duel's tier (see Opponents): four options from any pool in the first duel, six from the same pool in the last two. From tier 2 on the silence bar runs under the menu (see below).
3. Match: the counter wins, the attacker flinches, the defender gets the initiative and fills a segment. Miss: the defender flinches, the attacker keeps the initiative and fills a segment (two on a mic drop). On a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat before the flinch, so a lost exchange still teaches the pair.
4. Full buy-in ends the duel with win and lose poses and one bark each.

### Collecting: what the player knows

The player starts a run knowing their own role's eight pairs, phrase and counter (`You know your own lines. Everything else you pick up in the room.`). The other sixteen pairs are collected on defence: when an opponent throws a phrase the player has heard it, and the counter is shown either way, picked on a match or revealed on a miss. One exchange on the receiving end collects the whole pair, win or lose, so a lost duel still leaves the player better equipped for the retry, and no exchange is ever a dead end: the right counter is always in the menu, a miss is a guess that did not land.

Collecting changes two things and nothing else: the hand (below) draws from collected phrases only, and the glossary on the roster screen fills up. The defence menus, the bars, the mic drop and the boss are the same whether a pair is collected or not. Knowledge is per run: a second run as another role starts from that role's pool, so different opponents are easy and hard, and the three roles are three different runs rather than three copies of one.

Opponents help with the collecting without knowing it: every opponent throws from its whole pool, and on a first attempt prefers the pairs the player has not collected yet (see AI behaviour). A duel with three opponent attacks is three new pairs, so the four foreign duels of a run collect about twelve of the sixteen, and a replay of a beaten slot is a guaranteed gain, never a lottery. A full glossary needs a replay or two; that is what the roster's replay is for, and after a lost boss fight the loss bark says which column to fill.

The retry is the exception, because "unheard first" alone would make every retry a fresh exam. After a lost duel the opponent alternates, heard first: the first exchange of a retry is one the player can win from memory, which hands them the initiative and a momentum step, and the next throw is new. So a retry is earned and still collects one or two pairs. What is collected from a role pays off later, not against that role: Devs answer Dev lines every time, but those lines are the attack for the next role and the defence for the boss.

### Throwing a phrase: the hand

When the player has the initiative they do not get every phrase they know; they get a hand of four, drawn from the collected phrases not yet thrown in this duel, seeded per duel. A thrown phrase is spent for the duel and the hand refills from the rest on the next attack turn. Four fit on a phone, no two duels are the same, and the choice is real because of the next rule. The hand starts at eight phrases on duel one and grows towards 24 over the run; with fewer than four left the hand is simply smaller, and when a duel has used them all, spent phrases come back.

### Who knows what

An opponent's repertoire is its own pool plus one or two pairs it has picked up from the other roles (the "Also knows" column in Opponents: the Senior has heard "We don't have time to do it right" often enough to answer it). It throws from the whole repertoire and answers it every time; phrases outside it, it answers with the slot's accuracy (60 to 90 %). So on attack the player works out what the opponent does not know and throws that, and a hand with nothing the opponent is weak against is a bad hand to drop the mic from. The Stakeholder answers everything.

The borrowed pairs do two things. They make the own-role duels more than a memory test: the Head of Product throws your own lines, which you know, and then a Dev line you may not. And they seed the glossary across pools, so a Dev duel can hand you a Designer counter the boss will ask for later.

This is why the player meets their own role last: the pool they start with is foreign to the first two roles and lands hits from duel one, and by the time it is useless (against their own kind, who know it perfectly) they have collected the other two. The simulation (see Workbench) showed the other side of that: on defence the own-role duels are where memory gets tested, because you know that pool from the first second, and the foreign duels are where reasoning and collecting get tested. The slot table follows from it.

### The silence bar

From the third duel on the defender has a few seconds to answer. A thin bar under the menu fills while the player hesitates; when it is full the game answers for them with the fallback line `Uh… that's a good point.`, which counts as a miss. Memorised pairs still have to come out under pressure, which is what keeps tier 3 and the boss hard once the player knows the lines. Seconds per slot are in data (starting values: 10 s then 8 s at tier 2, 6 s at tier 3, 5 s against the boss, halved on a mic drop) and get tuned in the grey box.

The bar is off in the two tier 1 duels, off by default under `prefers-reduced-motion` and switchable on the title screen for screen reader users and anyone who wants a quiz instead of a duel. With the bar off every correct counter counts as fast for momentum.

### First duel

The first duel is the tutorial whoever walks in: four options from any pool, no silence bar, and on the first exchange the dialogue box shows the hint `Pick the reply that answers it`. Nothing else explains the game; the title screen's three lines and the bars do the rest.

### Difficulty knobs

All in data: the slot table (menu size, decoy source, silence seconds and foreign-pool accuracy per slot), each character's borrowed pairs, the draw of two per role, hand size, the starting pool, momentum steps, mic drop value, and the boss mapping. Nothing is hard-coded. The numbers below are the ones the simulation settled on for v1; the sandbox and real play move them.

## Player characters

The player picks one of three roles on the title screen, and one of two looks per role. The role changes the order of opponents and the boss's framing; the look only changes the sprite. The mechanic and the lines are the same for all six.

| Role     | Looks (working names)                                                                                               | Prop                 | Opponent order                                |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| PM       | Jo: tall, cropped coils, striped shirt, lanyard, notebook under the arm. Ravi: stocky, bald, beard, quarter-zip     | Sticky-note stack    | Dev team, then Designers, then PMs, then boss |
| Dev      | Sam: the existing sheet, blue hoodie, headphones. Lena: red ponytail, green zip hoodie open over a band shirt       | Laptop with stickers | Designers, then PMs, then Devs, then boss     |
| Designer | Ines: high bun, round glasses, black turtleneck, yellow socks. Theo: flat-top, gold-rimmed glasses, camel overshirt | Tablet with stylus   | PMs, then Devs, then Designers, then boss     |

Rules behind the table:

- The player always meets their own role last before the boss. Those lines are the ones you find funniest and least fair, your own pool is the one the opponent knows perfectly, so your attacks have to come from what you collected on the way, and on defence it is where your memory gets tested, with the borrowed pairs as the surprise. Tier belongs to the slot in the run, so whoever of your own role shows up is the tier 3 fight; the Intern who read the whole wiki is the point.
- Each role has a distinct silhouette: the prop does most of the work, the clothes and the head shape the rest. The two looks of a role share the prop and the silhouette and differ in everything else: face, hair, skin, build, age, clothes. They are two people, not a palette swap, so each is its own model sheet. See Art and animation.
- The nine colleagues are swaps of these six base sheets (which base each uses is in the swap table), so the cast is as mixed as the players.
- Names are placeholders and only appear in the title screen and the save; duellists never use them in dialogue so lines stay role-neutral.
- Role choice is stored with the save. Switching role starts a new run; the old run is kept under its role so the player can finish all three (that is the completionist hook: one line of credits per finished role, three result cards, three full glossaries).

Selection screen: three roles side by side, each shown as one sprite in idle pose on a plain dark background, the active one lit, name and a one-line blurb below (`Has a roadmap and is not afraid to use it.` / `Will fix it in the next sprint. Which sprint is a detail.` / `Has opinions about your line height.`). Left/right or tap picks the role, up/down or a tap on the sprite flips it to the role's other look (both names shown, the active one lit), Enter or tap again confirms. The look is stored with the run.

### The run

A run is seven slots: six colleagues and the Stakeholder. Each run draws two of the three characters per role at random, seeded per run, and puts them in the player's role order (see Player characters): two of the first foreign role, two of the second, two of their own. The third character of each role is out of office this run and rotates in on the next one. The slot sets everything the old tier did:

| Slot | Role in the run     | Tier | Menu | Decoys from | Silence | Outside its repertoire |
| ---- | ------------------- | ---- | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ---------------------- |
| 1    | First foreign role  | 1    | 4    | any pool    | off     | 60 %                   |
| 2    | First foreign role  | 1    | 4    | any pool    | off     | 60 %                   |
| 3    | Second foreign role | 2    | 5    | any pool    | 10 s    | 70 %                   |
| 4    | Second foreign role | 2    | 5    | any pool    | 8 s     | 75 %                   |
| 5    | Own role            | 3    | 6    | same pool   | 6 s     | 90 %                   |
| 6    | Own role            | 3    | 6    | same pool   | 6 s     | 90 %                   |
| 7    | The Stakeholder     | Boss | 6    | all pools   | 5 s     | 100 %                  |

"Decoys from" is where the wrong counters in the player's defence menu are drawn from: any pool means the wrong answers are obviously from another conversation, same pool means they all sound like plausible replies. Plausible decoys test memory and obvious decoys test reasoning, so the decoys follow what the player knows of the pool: the foreign pools, fresh, get obvious decoys and the pressure comes from the timer and the accuracy; the own pool, known from the first second, gets plausible ones. ("Two pools", the phrase's own pool plus one other, stays in the data as a step between the two but is unused in v1.) A mic drop sets the menu to eight whatever the slot. "Outside its repertoire" is the AI's accuracy on phrases it has not got; its repertoire it answers every time.

With these numbers the simulation's modelled player (recalls a collected pair 90 % of the time) wins a first attempt at 45, 39, 31, 35, 60, 61 and 93 % across the slots, and a retry at about 70 % and up; a weaker player (70 % recall) sits at 35 to 45 % everywhere and 66 % at the boss. The foreign duels are a steady third to a half, the own-role duels and the boss move with how well the player knows the lines.

The ramp is fixed and the faces are random, which is the point: the first duel is always the tutorial whoever walks in, and the run never opens with a silence bar. A run is 12 to 15 minutes.

Between duels: a single roster screen over the darkened corner office, the seven slots as a vertical list in run order with the character's sprite, name and a tick when beaten; a second mark (a star) when beaten flawless, that is without losing an exchange. The out-of-office characters are listed greyed at the bottom with `Out of office`, so the player sees the cast is bigger than the run. The next unbeaten slot is selected, beaten ones can be replayed for the star or to collect what they throw, the Stakeholder is greyed until all six ticks are there. Enter or tap starts the duel; Escape returns to the title.

The glossary: a second tab on the roster (Tab or a tap on `Glossary 11/24` in the corner), three columns of eight slots, one per pool. A collected pair shows phrase and counter; an uncollected one shows a blank line in its pool's column, so the player can see which role still has lines to throw and which slot to replay. The starting pool is collected from the first second. The count goes on the result card and is the reason to replay a beaten slot when the roster is already full of ticks.

### The Stakeholder

- Only reachable after all six slots are beaten (the save tracks a flag per slot).
- Never gives up the initiative. The Stakeholder's buy-in bar is full when the fight starts (the joke: they don't need yours), and the whole duel is a barrage of the six boss phrases, one after the other, in random order, no regular phrases.
- The player's buy-in bar has four segments. Every counter fills one; three misses lose. The maths closes on the sixth phrase: four right and two misses is a win, three misses is a loss, so the last phrase always decides when it gets that far.
- Every boss phrase is answered by one regular counter. The mapping is fixed in data and the same for every role. The defence menu's six options draw decoys from all three pools, so the player has to recognise the reply in a new context (the Monkey Island Sword Master trick: known counters, new phrases).
- Momentum is the only way to get a word in. With a full bar the player interrupts: they throw one phrase from a hand of four, the Stakeholder doesn't look up, says `Let's take this offline.`, and the player fills two segments. Three fast counters then the interrupt is the flawless boss win in four exchanges, and that bark is the reward for the hardest fight.
- On loss, the boss bark names the pool of the counter the player last got wrong (`Have you talked to engineering about this?` when it was a Dev line, and so on). Hints are the only help, and this one is a pointer: that pool's column in the glossary has the blanks, and that role's slots are a tap away on the roster.
- The boss can test a pair the player never heard; the counter is in the menu like anywhere else, so it is a guess, not a dead end. Losing to it once and knowing why is the intended experience. The boss is not locked behind a full glossary; that would turn the replay from a choice into homework.

### The ending

No twist about who the Stakeholder is. "The stakeholder was the CEO" is what every reader assumes from the suit and the phone, so it would be a confirmation, not a reveal, and it would turn a joke about meetings into one about hierarchy, which breaks the tone rule above. The reveal is about the player instead:

- **The losing line is keyed to the player's role.** The Stakeholder used to be one of you: `Fine. Put it in the next steering deck. I used to write those.` for a PM, `… I used to review those.` for a Dev, `… I used to draw those.` for a Designer. Three variants of one bark, no art, and it lands because everyone has met the ex-developer in the corner office. The joke is that you were playing against your own future.
- **The phone**, if it reads at native size (decided in the polish package): when the Stakeholder finally looks up after the loss, the glowing phone screen on the desk, already a living detail of the scene, shows the Pushback title screen for one beat. They were playing it the whole time. One small sprite swap in the loop script, self-referential, and keeps "merely busy".

### AI behaviour

On attack: pick a random phrase from the opponent's pool, no repeats within a duel until all are used, then any. The pick order depends on the attempt: on a first attempt at a slot, and on a replay of a beaten slot, uncollected pairs first (collecting); on a retry after a loss, alternate collected and uncollected, collected first (the earned retry). Within each group the pick is random. With full momentum, slots at tier 1 and 2 drop the mic at once, tier 3 holds it until the hit would end the duel. On defence: own pool always right; foreign pools right with the slot's accuracy, otherwise a random wrong counter from the phrase's pool. The Stakeholder never guesses and never attacks with a mic drop (every boss phrase is already worth a miss).

## Backgrounds

Four scenes, one per opponent role and one for the boss, all made with the `pixel-header` pipeline: Gemini prompt with a header as style reference, `snap.mjs` to the native grid, a per-scene loop script for two or three living details. Target 480 by 270 native pixels (16:9, no 5:2 crop this time; a 1376 px Gemini render snaps at its native 3 px grid to about 458 wide, and `sheet.mjs` pads it to 480 by 270 by repeating the plain edge columns and rows, which is why the edges must stay plain), the same 32-colour, 3 to 4 shades per material look as the headers. Scenes carry no people; the sprites are the people.

| Scene           | Used for           | Time and palette                                     | Where the duellists stand                                              | Living details (looped)                                                             |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Open-plan floor | Dev opponents      | Late afternoon, blue-grey with warm monitor light    | Between two desk islands, monitors behind them as plain dark areas     | A monitor flicker, a CI light blinking green then red, a plant leaf in the AC draft |
| Meeting room    | PM opponents       | Mid-morning, beige and whiteboard white              | In front of the whiteboard, table in the foreground framing the bottom | Projector fan dust in the beam, a coffee mug steaming, the whiteboard marker light  |
| Studio corner   | Designer opponents | Evening, warm wood and one cool desk lamp            | Beside a moodboard wall, printer on the left as a dark frame           | Lamp flicker, a sticky note lifting in a draft, a tablet screen pulsing on standby  |
| Corner office   | The Stakeholder    | Night, city lights behind glass, dark navy and amber | Desk between the duellists, window behind                              | City windows twinkling, phone screen glowing on the desk, rain streaks on the glass |

Composition rule for all four: the middle band from y=60 to y=225 stays uncluttered where the sprites stand (x=60 to 170 for the left duellist, x=310 to 420 for the right). The top 40 px is a plain ceiling or sky and holds the two bars per duellist; the bottom 45 px a plain floor; the dialogue box overlays the floor. Dark framing shapes on the left and right edges, as in the headers.

Title and selection screens reuse the corner office at a darker exposure (no new scene).

Prompt: the `pixel-header` template as is, with two changes. The composition paragraph becomes `16:9 frame, about 480 pixels wide, with an empty plain middle band where two figures will stand later`, and `No people` stays. Living details are staged exactly as the skill says: in front of plain dark areas so the loop script can draw them cleanly.

Output per scene: `native.png`, `scene.webp` (lossless, 8 fps, 12 s loop) and `poster.png` for reduced motion, in `src/assets/lab/pushback/scenes/<slug>/`. Unlike the headers, the Gemini original is kept, in `src/assets/lab/pushback/sources/`, as the reference for later changes to the scene.

## Lines

Three pools of eight phrase/counter pairs (one pool per role), six boss pairs that reuse regular counters, and the barks. 24 regular pairs (48 lines), 6 boss phrases, 22 barks and fallbacks: 76 lines in total, all first drafts to be edited in this table. Rules for every line: role-neutral (never names the target's role, so any player can be on the receiving end), under 70 characters so it fits two rows of the dialogue box, no names, and the counter must read as a reply even out of context (the decoy menus and the boss depend on that).

Who throws what: every character throws from its whole pool, uncollected pairs first, except on a retry (see AI behaviour). The player's hand draws from what they have collected.

## Art and animation

Nothing is drawn by hand. Gemini generates every image (free tier), `snap.mjs` pulls it onto the pixel grid, scripts in the repo do the compositing, palette work and animation, and Claude Code reviews and repairs the pixels. Sprites stay at the pixel size Gemini draws them. The Dev sheet of 26 October (`sources/dev-sheet.jpg`, a 1376 by 768 render) snaps at a 6 px grid to figures about 74 px tall, 80 with the laptop raised; the height range for sheets is 70 to 85 px on a 72 by 112 cell, and roles must not differ in height for no reason (a sheet outside the range is rerolled, not scaled). Downscaling to 48 px lost the face and the headphones, so the stage is 480 by 270; a figure is then a bit under a third of the screen height, the classic adventure-game proportion. Same palette discipline as the headers (3 to 4 shades per material, dark outline on the silhouette only), same pixel size as the scene.

Why Gemini and not a sprite tool: the AI sprite services (PixelLab and similar) are paid, and the Claude Code pixel plugins only script Aseprite, which is paid or a self-compile; neither draws a better figure than a reference-steered Gemini prompt. Gemini holds a character steady inside one image, so every character is generated as a single model sheet, not frame by frame.

The prompts for every image are in `docs/plans/pushback-prompts.md`: twelve generations before rerolls (six model sheets, four scenes, one prop sheet, one head sheet; the Dev man's sheet exists), each with the references to attach, the file name under `sources/` and what to reroll on.

Pipeline per character:

1. Prompt Gemini for one model sheet: the figure six times in a row (idle, talk-open, taunt, flinch, win, lose), same character, same scale, front three-quarter view, plain dark background, a header attached as the style reference, the pixel-header template with `No people` removed and `Six copies of the same figure side by side, identical in size and colours, each in a different pose` added. Reroll until the six are the same person. PM, Designer and Stakeholder use the same prompt with the Dev sheet attached as a second reference, so scale and pixel size match.
2. `snap.mjs` at the sheet's detected grid (6 px on the Dev render), nothing forced; all six poses share one grid because they come from one image.
3. `sheet.mjs` cuts the strip into 72 by 112 cells by detecting the gaps, aligns each pose on its feet line, checks the figure height, keys out the background, locks the palette to the scene's 32 colours and writes the sheet plus a 4x contact print.
4. Claude Code reviews the contact print (it can view the PNG) against the checklist in the `pixel-sprite` skill: same height across poses, no stray single pixels, mouth and prop readable, palette within the scene. It fixes small faults in code (`sheet.mjs` has `setPx`-style helpers as in `loop.mjs`) and sends larger faults back to step 1 with a follow-up prompt in the same Gemini chat (`Keep this exactly, but …`).

Opponents are palette and prop swaps done in code, not new drawings: two model sheets per role (the two looks the player can pick) plus one for the Stakeholder, so seven Gemini sheets in total, and each colleague is a swap of one of its role's two bases. A swap is a colour lookup table in the character data plus an optional prop cell pasted at a fixed anchor; the eight props come from one Gemini item sheet cut on its gaps by `sheet.mjs`, like the model sheets. Where a swap needs a head change (hood up, beard), the head comes from one head sheet generated with the Dev sheet attached as reference, cut the same way. The Gemini originals are kept in the repo as `src/assets/lab/pushback/sources/<id>.jpg`; files under `src/assets` only ship when imported, so nothing excludes them from the build, they are simply never imported. They are the reference image for every later prompt, and a snapped PNG works worse as one.

Animation is code, not frames:

| Pose   | Source             | How it moves                                                                                                             |
| ------ | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Idle   | Model sheet cell 1 | 1 px vertical bob every 600 ms, seeded like the header loops                                                             |
| Talk   | Cells 1 and 2      | Alternate at 120 ms while the line types; a third mouth state is cell 1 with the mouth rows darkened by one shade        |
| Taunt  | Cell 3             | Hold, plus 2 px step toward the opponent over 2 frames; on a mic drop the step is 4 px and the `Mic drop!` label appears |
| Flinch | Cell 4             | Hold 400 ms, 2 px step back, one frame of a one-shade-lighter overlay                                                    |
| Win    | Cell 5             | Hold, 1 px bob at double speed                                                                                           |
| Lose   | Cell 6             | Hold, no bob                                                                                                             |

If a sheet comes back with only four usable poses, the fallback is flinch for lose and taunt for win; the spec allows it and the data marks which cells exist. The right-hand duellist is the sheet mirrored; props with text are avoided in the prompts for that reason. The Stakeholder never leaves idle or talk during the barrage; the interrupt is the one time they flinch, and the lose pose is the one time they look up.

UI chrome is drawn in code, not generated: the dialogue box as a 9-slice from a few palette colours, the buy-in bar (three or four segments, 10 by 6 px each) with the momentum bar below it (three 6 by 3 px steps), the silence bar as a 1 px line under the menu, and a pointing-hand cursor as a 12 by 12 bitmap in the source. Rectangles this small are cleaner typed than snapped from a render, and they take their colours from the scene's palette so they sit in every room. A full momentum bar blinks at the idle bob's rhythm. Text is not drawn into the canvas: the dialogue line, the menus, the hand, the barks and the `Mic drop!` label are DOM in the site's Fira Code, laid over the stage like the hotspot remarks under the note headers (`font-mono`, small, with the same one-pixel text shadow). The overlay text never goes below 12 px, so at 1x on a phone the dialogue box and menu grow past the drawn chrome; the 70-character limit in Lines fits two rows at every integer scale from 2x up.

Rendering: scene, sprites and chrome are composited into a 480 by 270 canvas with `imageSmoothingEnabled = false`, scaled to the largest integer factor that fits the viewport (2x on laptops, 3x on 1080p, 4x on 1440p, 1x on phones in landscape with a rotate hint in portrait). The DOM overlay is positioned in the same scaled frame. `loop.mjs` gets a `scene-1x.webp` export so scenes and sprites share one pixel grid.

## Tech

The game is a client component on a new route in the existing Next.js app; no new framework, no game engine. The repo's stack (Next 16, React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind 4, oxlint, Node test runner) covers everything, and the state machine is plain enough to unit-test without a browser.

Data shapes (the spec's tables map 1:1 onto these):

```typescript
type Role = 'pm' | 'dev' | 'designer';

type Pair = { id: string; role: Role; phrase: string; counter: string };
type BossPair = { id: string; phrase: string; answeredBy: Pair['id'] };

type Tier = 1 | 2 | 3 | 'boss';

type Character = {
  id: string;
  name: string;
  role: Role | 'boss';
  sheet: {
    base: Role | 'boss';
    head?: string;
    prop?: string;
    accessory?: string;
  };
  palette?: Record<string, string>;
  scene: 'floor' | 'meeting' | 'studio' | 'office';
  intro: string;
};

type Slot = {
  tier: Tier;
  accuracy: number; // on foreign pools; own pool is always 1
  menu: number; // counter options on defence
  decoys: 'any' | 'two' | 'same' | 'all';
  silence: number | null; // seconds, null = off
};

type Run = {
  seed: number;
  slots: { character: Character['id']; slot: Slot }[]; // 6 colleagues + the boss
};

type Rules = {
  buyIn: number; // 3, boss duel 4
  momentum: number; // 3
  micDrop: { worth: number; menu: number; silence: number }; // 2, 8, 0.5
  hand: number; // 4
  bossMisses: number; // 3
};

type RunStats = {
  exchangesWon: number;
  exchangesLost: number;
  micDrops: number;
  missesByPool: Record<Role, number>;
};

type Save = {
  version: 1;
  silence: boolean; // the silence bar switch on the title screen
  runs: Partial<
    Record<
      Role,
      {
        run: Run; // the draw, so a reload keeps the same cast
        beaten: Record<number, { flawless: boolean }>; // by slot index
        collected: Pair['id'][]; // starts as the role's own pool
        finished: boolean;
        stats: RunStats;
      }
    >
  >;
};
```

Duel state machine (one reducer, screens derived from `phase`):

1. `intro`: opponent bark, then `attack` with the opponent holding the initiative (the challenger always opens). The duel starts with an `attempt` of `first`, `retry` (the slot was lost before and is not beaten) or `replay` (the slot is beaten), which sets the AI's pick order.
2. `attack`: attacker's line types out. Player attacker picks from the hand of four and, with full momentum, toggles the mic drop first; AI picks per AI behaviour.
3. `defend`: counter menu (player, with the silence bar sending `tick` actions and a `timeout` that plays the fallback) or AI pick. Resolves to `resolve`.
4. `resolve`: taunt/flinch poses, buy-in segment fills, momentum updates; on a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat. A pair the player just defended against is added to `collected`. Initiative to the winner. Full buy-in → `end`, else `attack`.
5. `end`: win/lose poses and barks (the boss's loss bark keyed to the pool of the last missed counter), save written with the stats, then back to the roster or, after the boss, the result card and the credits line.

The boss duel runs the same reducer with `buyIn: 4`, the initiative pinned to the boss, a miss counter that ends the duel at three, and the interrupt as the one `attack` phase the player gets, entered from `resolve` when momentum is full.

Rendering loop: a single `requestAnimationFrame` loop at 8 fps steps (125 ms, matching the scene loops) draws scene frame, left sprite, right sprite and chrome into the 480 by 270 canvas. Dialogue and menus are real DOM (text and buttons overlaid on the canvas, styled to match) so they are accessible and tappable; the canvas is decorative and gets `aria-hidden`, the current line is also written to a visually hidden live region, and the bars have text equivalents there (`Buy-in 2 of 3, momentum full`).

Input: arrow keys or W/S to move in menus, Enter or Space to confirm and to skip typing, M to toggle the mic drop when available, Escape to pause. Touch: tap a menu item, tap the stage to skip typing, tap the momentum bar to toggle the mic drop. The whole thing works with a screen reader through the DOM menus and the live region, with the silence bar off.

Reduced motion: scenes show the poster, idle bob and talk cycle are off, typing is instant, the silence bar defaults to off. Same rule as the headers.

Tests: `duel.ts` and `ai.ts` under the Node test runner (buy-in and initiative, momentum gains and resets, mic drop worth and cost, the hand draws from collected phrases only and never repeats one within a duel, a run starts with exactly the role's own pool collected and a defended pair is collected win or lose, the AI throws uncollected pairs first on a first attempt and a replay and alternates from a collected pair on a retry, a run draws two distinct characters per role in the role order and the slot table applies by position, decoy rules and menu size per slot, own-pool answers are always right, the silence timeout plays the fallback as a miss, the boss barrage ends on the sixth phrase under every outcome, the interrupt fires only at full momentum, boss mapping completeness: every boss phrase has exactly one answering pair that exists; every line under 70 characters; the result card text matches the stats). `sheets.test.ts` validates the committed sheets. One Playwright VRT screenshot of the title screen and one of a frozen duel frame, added to the existing visual suite (ask before running VRT, per `AGENTS.md`).

### Workbench

Tests are enough for the rules and not enough for anything you look at. The reducer, the AI, the draw and the save are pure and the tests above pin them down; what a test cannot tell is whether a 74-pixel sprite reads at 2x, whether six seconds of silence is tense or cruel, whether the `Mic drop!` label lands on the right frame, or whether a palette swap is the wrong shade. Playing through to the slot in question to find out is a tuning loop measured in minutes, so the game gets a workbench. Not a second site: a dev-only route in the same app, `/lab/pushback/dev`, which calls `notFound()` in production. Same bundler, Tailwind, fonts, asset imports and fast refresh as the game, no parallel config for the one thing that makes the pixels right (integer scaling and the asset pipeline). No Storybook, no Vite page.

Three panels:

- **Sprite viewer.** Any character, any pose, 1x to 4x, with the animation table running and the swaps applied; a toggle for the mirrored right-hand side. The contact print from `sheet.mjs` is the still version of this; the viewer is where motion and swaps get checked.
- **Scene viewer.** Each scene with its loop and its poster, at the same scales, with the stand positions and the clear band drawn as outlines so a new render can be checked against the composition rule.
- **Duel sandbox.** Pick character and slot, set a seed, jump to any phase, fill momentum, force the barrage, a slider for the silence seconds, a reduced-motion toggle. Because the reducer is pure, "jump to phase" is constructing a state and handing it to the same stage component the game uses, so the sandbox exercises the real thing, not a copy.

One script beside it, `simulate.ts`, run with the same tsx loader as the tests: a modelled player (answers collected pairs with a given accuracy, guesses the rest at one over the menu size, respects the silence bar with a given speed) plays full runs against the slot table, a thousand seeded runs per setting, and prints win rate per slot on first attempts and on retries (a retry must win more often than a first attempt, or the loop is not earned), average duels to beat the boss, and glossary size when the boss is reached. That is how the 60/75/90, the silence seconds and the boss's four segments get set with numbers before anyone plays, and it is the check that the boss is a wall for a player who knows nothing and not for one who collected the room.

The workbench grows with the packages: the simulation in package 1 (it only needs the rules), the sandbox with the grey box in package 3, the sprite and scene viewers as the assets land in 4 and 5.

Only if v1 feels thin after a few weeks of play; both are small once the rules are in data.
