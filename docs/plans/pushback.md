# Pushback: Game Spec

Oct 26, 2026 · @Sebastian · revised Oct 27

## Overview

Pushback is a small browser game on sebald.me: a duel of phrases and counters between the roles of a product team, in the pixel-art style of the note headers. The player picks a role (PM, Dev or Designer), works through a gauntlet of colleagues and faces the Stakeholder as the final boss. Each exchange is won by picking the right counter to the phrase just thrown, or by throwing a phrase the opponent cannot answer; the duel is won by filling your buy-in bar first. The player starts knowing their own role's lines and picks up everyone else's in the room.

The three rules on the title screen: Counter the phrase. Push the scale to your side. Full momentum lets you drop the mic.

Scope for v1:

- One route, `/lab/pushback`, no backend, no accounts; progress lives in `localStorage`.
- Three playable roles, a cast of nine colleagues (three per role) of which each run draws one per role, one boss. A run is four duels, about five minutes, and meant to be played again: the lines and the faces are rolled fresh each time.
- 168 lines of dialogue (48 phrase/counter pairs, 9 closers, 12 boss phrases, 24 barks and fallbacks), all original text; a run plays a deck of 24 pairs drawn from the 48. The mechanic is borrowed from the Monkey Island insult sword fighting, the words are not. The collecting is kept in a form that fits a mini game: the player's own pool is known from the start, the other two are collected by hearing them, and a lost exchange is never a dead end. The two bars are borrowed from fighting games and that is where the borrowing stops.
- Four backgrounds made with the existing `pixel-header` pipeline, character sprites generated by Gemini at their native pixel size.
- Keyboard and touch playable, a 320 by 214 stage shown at 3x (960 by 642) wherever it fits and shrunk with the viewport below that.
- A result card after the boss that can be copied as text.

Out of scope for v1: sound (the repo has an audio folder, but music is a v2 question), a map or overworld, multiplayer, localisation (English only, like the blog), and any analytics beyond the existing Vercel counter. Role passives and a daily seed are listed under After v1.

Tone: dry and affectionate. Every role gets roasted and every role gets to roast back. Nobody is the villain, not even the Stakeholder, who is merely very busy.

## Core mechanic

A duel is a sequence of exchanges. The attacker throws a phrase, the defender picks a counter from a short menu; the right counter wins the exchange and swaps the initiative, a miss keeps it with the attacker. Every won exchange pushes the shared scale one step toward the winner. Pushing it to your edge wins the duel.

### The scale and the momentum bars

One scale between the duellists at the top of the stage, and a momentum bar in each corner:

- **The scale** (the pushback). It starts in the middle and every won exchange moves the marker one step toward the winner, two for a mic drop, landed or countered. Three steps to either edge win the duel (four for the player against the boss, three for the boss). It swings both ways, so a losing streak can turn round and every exchange visibly moves something. An earlier version had a buy-in bar per side that only filled; with three segments and three momentum steps the mic drop could never happen, since the third win already ended the duel.
- **Fatigue.** Two good duellists would trade their own defences for ever: each counters their own pool every time, so the marker only wobbles. So the opponent runs out of comebacks: its chance to counter anything, own pool included, drops ten points per exchange (five in hard mode). Nothing is on a clock and nobody is declared a winner by the bell; the scale always reaches an edge. The simulation puts duels at six to nine exchanges, with a mic drop in most tier-1 duels and about one in three later.
- **Momentum**, two steps. Playing well fills it, losing an exchange empties it. Full momentum puts a closer on offer, the mic drop; at two steps it is reachable before the duel is over.

| Event                                                         | Momentum                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Counter before the silence bar is half full                   | +1                                  |
| Counter after that (or with the silence bar off: any counter) | +1 only when the bar is off, else 0 |
| Landed phrase (the opponent missed)                           | +1                                  |
| Lost exchange (missed counter or a countered phrase)          | reset to 0                          |

### The mic drop

With full momentum and the initiative, the attacker can drop the mic. The mic drop is a line of its own: each role has three closers (see Lines), louder than the pool's phrases, and with a full bar one of them joins the player's hand as a fifth option, in gold and blinking at the arcade rhythm, under the prompt `Full momentum. The gold line drops the mic.` Throwing it is the drop; throwing a phrase from the hand instead keeps the bar. (An earlier version toggled the drop on and off with an `M` key and a `Mic drop: on` item in the hand, a setting among lines, which nobody found and everybody forgot.) A dropped closer moves the scale two steps instead of one, toward the attacker when it lands and toward the defender when it is countered, so it is a gamble. It costs the whole momentum bar and raises the stakes for the defender: the counter menu grows to eight and the silence bar runs at three quarters. The opponent drops the mic the same way, with one of its role's closers, telegraphed by the taunt pose and the burst, so a full enemy momentum bar is a visible threat.

Which closer is on offer follows from the duel's seed and moves on with every mic drop thrown, so the same one stays until it is used and a duel offers all three before repeating. Closers are outside every deck and repertoire: nobody answers one from memory, a colleague counters it at the slot's accuracy less fatigue, own role or not, so the own-role duel is no trap and the gamble is the same against every colleague. The Stakeholder doesn't look up (the interrupt). The player knows their own three from the start and collects the foreign six on defence like any pair; the glossary lists them under each pool.

Presentation: a mic drop is the loudest thing in the game and looks it. A comic burst, a jagged pixel starburst with MIC DROP lettered inside it (one Gemini sheet, see the prompt pack: the burst, the burst a step bigger with the letters tilted, and the popped burst), appears in the band between the two duellists at head height, so it never covers a face, and pulses between its two cells at 150 ms while the phrase types. The band flashes one frame of white under it and shakes two pixels for two frames, and the thrower takes the 4 px taunt step. While the exchange is live the burst sits still at the same place, replacing the old `Mic drop!` badge; on the resolve it pulses again three times when the phrase lands, or snaps to the popped cell and drops out of the band when it is countered. Under reduced motion the burst appears and leaves without the pulse, the flash and the shake. The lettering is part of the sprite rather than DOM text, so it reads as pixel art and can be mangled with the burst.

For the AI as defender the menu size makes no difference, so a player's mic drop against it is simply worth two; the decision it leaves the player is when to spend it, since a tired colleague late in the duel counters less. Momentum resets on any lost exchange, so holding a full bar for the perfect moment is the risk and spending it now is the safe play.

### The exchange, step by step

1. Attacker steps forward, line types out at 25 characters per second (tap or Space to complete instantly), then holds long enough to be read once more before the next step; the poses of a resolved exchange hold a second and a half.
2. Defender gets a menu of counters in random order: the one that answers the phrase plus decoys. Menu size and decoy source depend on the duel's tier (see Opponents): four options from any pool in the first duel, six from the same pool in the last two. From tier 2 on the silence bar runs under the menu (see below).
3. Match: the counter wins, the attacker flinches (a flash, then a second of the figure flickering in and out every frame, the arcade mark of a landed hit), the defender gets the initiative and pushes the scale a step. Miss: the defender flinches, the attacker keeps the initiative and pushes a step (two on a mic drop). On a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat before the flinch, so a lost exchange still teaches the pair.
4. The scale at an edge ends the duel with win and lose poses and one bark each.

### Collecting: what the player knows

Each pool has sixteen pairs; a run plays eight of each, its deck (`drawDeck`, seeded per run: of each pool two pairs whose counter answers a boss phrase, so the Stakeholder has lines, and six of the rest, so no two runs read the same). The player starts a run knowing their own role's eight pairs of the deck, phrase and counter (`You know your own lines. Everything else you pick up in the room.`). The other sixteen are collected on defence: when an opponent throws a phrase the player has heard it, and the counter is shown either way, picked on a match or revealed on a miss. One exchange on the receiving end collects the whole pair, win or lose, so a lost duel still leaves the player better equipped for the retry, and no exchange is ever a dead end: the right counter is always in the menu, a miss is a guess that did not land.

Collecting changes two things and nothing else: the hand (below) draws from collected phrases only, and the glossary fills up. The defence menus, the bars, the mic drop and the boss are the same whether a pair is collected or not. Knowledge is per run: every run starts cold with a fresh deck, nothing learnt carries into play, so the three roles are three different runs rather than three copies of one and a second run as the same role is new too. The glossary is the one thing that outlives a run: the save keeps every pair ever collected, as a record, and it fills towards 48 over several runs.

Opponents help with the collecting without knowing it: every opponent throws from its whole pool, and on a first attempt prefers the pairs the player has not collected yet (see AI behaviour). A duel with three opponent attacks is three new pairs, so the two foreign duels of a run collect about ten of the sixteen (the simulation says 17 to 18 of the deck's 24 at the boss), and a replay of a beaten slot is a guaranteed gain, never a lottery. A full deck needs a replay or two; that is what the roster's replay is for, and after a lost boss fight the loss bark says which column to fill.

The retry is the exception, because "unheard first" alone would make every retry a fresh exam. After a lost duel the opponent alternates, heard first: the first exchange of a retry is one the player can win from memory, which hands them the initiative and a momentum step, and the next throw is new. So a retry is earned and still collects one or two pairs. What is collected from a role pays off later, not against that role: Devs answer Dev lines every time, but those lines are the attack for the next role and the defence for the boss.

### Throwing a phrase: the hand

When the player has the initiative they do not get every phrase they know; they get a hand of four, drawn from the collected phrases not yet thrown in this duel, seeded per duel. A thrown phrase is spent for the duel and the hand refills from the rest on the next attack turn. Four fit on a phone, no two duels are the same, and the choice is real because of the next rule. The hand starts at eight phrases on duel one and grows towards 24 over the run; with fewer than four left the hand is simply smaller, and when a duel has used them all, spent phrases come back.

### Who knows what

An opponent's repertoire is its own pool of the deck plus one or two pairs it has picked up from the other roles (the `knows` count in the cast: the Senior has heard a PM line often enough to answer it; which pairs follows from the run's deck and seed, `repertoireFor`, so a retry meets the same colleague). It throws from the whole repertoire and answers it every time; phrases outside it, it answers with the slot's accuracy (60 to 85 %). So on attack the player works out what the opponent does not know and throws that. The Stakeholder answers everything, closers aside.

The borrowed pairs do two things. They make the own-role duel more than a memory test: the Head of Product throws your own lines, which you know, and then a Dev line you may not. And they seed the glossary across pools, so a Dev duel can hand you a Designer counter the boss will ask for later.

The player meets their own role first: the first duel is on home ground, the pool known from the first second, so the mechanics are learnt on lines the player can answer, and the two foreign duels after it are where reasoning and collecting get tested. The simulation (see Workbench) showed that split: on defence the own-role duel tests memory, the foreign ones test reasoning. The slot table follows from it.

### The silence bar

From the second duel on, in normal and hard mode (easy has no bar), the defender has a few seconds to answer. A thin bar under the menu fills while the player hesitates; when it is full the game answers for them with the fallback line `Uh… that's a good point.`, which counts as a miss. Memorised pairs still have to come out under pressure, which is what keeps tier 3 and the boss hard once the player knows the lines.

The bar's length follows the menu's text, not the slot (`silenceLimit`): a counter is eight words on average, adults read about 240 words a minute (Brysbaert 2019, a meta-analysis of silent reading), and the right counter sits halfway down the list on average, so the bar is a share of the time a median reader needs for the whole menu: 1.1 of it in normal mode, 0.7 in hard mode (`RULES.reading`), times 0.75 on a mic drop. A menu of six is about fifty words, twelve seconds to read, so the bar runs about thirteen seconds in normal mode and eight and a half in hard; the mic drop's eight options give the same. Normal played at 0.8 first and was too stressful; at 1.1 it gives a median reader the whole menu and a little more. Fixed seconds per slot were tried first and hard mode's 0.6 of them was unreadable: 3.6 seconds for eight options, a fifth of the text; 0.6 and 0.5 of the reading time came next and played two or three seconds too short.

The bar is off in the first duel. With the bar off every correct counter counts as fast for momentum.

### Modes

One switch on the title screen, `Mode: Easy`, `Mode: Normal` or `Mode: Hard` in turn, kept in the save; a new save starts on easy, since the ghost is the onboarding. A run is played in one mode, so its score means one thing: switching starts over, every run and the glossary gone (`setMode`), and with anything at stake it asks first in a dialog (`Confirm`: the stage dimmed, a quiet window with what it costs, `Cancel` selected and `Switch to hard` under it, Escape cancels). Reset asks in the same dialog. The ghost and the timer are different help, so each mode changes one thing: easy has the ghost (below) whisper along and no silence bar (`Rules.timer` off: nobody is rushed and every won defence counts as fast); normal has no ghost and the bar at 1.1 of the reading time; hard has no ghost, the bar at 0.7, two more options on every menu (the boss's included), colleagues who tire at seven points instead of ten, and a Stakeholder who leaves after two misses instead of three. The score counts a mode's worth (`Rules.score`: easy 1, normal 1.5, hard 2, see Result card). The simulation (`MODE=easy` or `MODE=hard`) cannot see most of that, since it does not model reading time or the ghost; the difference is for people. Flawless stars are only earned without the ghost, in normal and hard, because the ghost is a safety net. A save from the two-mode version reads its normal as easy, since that normal had the ghost.

### The onboarding buddy

The ghost of the player's predecessor in the role whispers the reply, slowly, in easy mode (see Modes). While the player hesitates on defence, the wrong counters dim in three steps until only the right one is bright (a first version kept one decoy bright for a two-way call at the end; it read as the ghost being wrong, and went). The steps land at 50, 70 and 88 percent of the silence limit (or of eight seconds when the bar is off): the ghost never shows while a fast answer is still possible, and the last step comes before the fallback.

The ghost is a character, not a setting. It is off stage until the first step, then a small black hole opens on the top-right corner of the window the counters are picked in, where the eye is while the player reads them, and spits it out (about half a second, the sprite squeezed sideways out of a dark disc with a pale rim), floating at about half the people's size, its tail resting on the window's frame, facing the options and pointing at them; it sits sunk twenty-six pixels into that corner, over the right end of the window's first rows, and says one line in a comic speech bubble over its head (`Bubble`: white paper, ink letters, an ink outline with the corners stepped a pixel at a time, a row of dithered shade along the bottom like the window's, and a stair-stepped tail pointing down to it; a dark teal bubble came first and read as a second window), placed on its own over the room and a little over the opponent, so it never moves the window's text (it sat in the window's prompt row before, and the text jumped when it appeared) (it first stood behind the player's shoulder, then in the gap between the duellists with the bubble over its head; there the player, reading the options, did not look up to see it); it stays until the player answers or the fallback fires, then the hole swallows it again. It helps once a duel: after the exchange it whispered on it is gone and the counters stop dimming. It speaks once per appearance, not on every step, so a slow player is not nagged three times an exchange. The lines are helpful and rude, in turn from a start set by the duel's seed so a duel never repeats one before it has used them all:

- `Let me help you, new hire.`
- `I had this one on my second day.`
- `Read it again. Slowly. I have time. I have nothing but time.`
- `I'm not haunting you. I'm onboarding you.`
- after a miss despite the whisper, during the resolve: `I dimmed them. I literally dimmed them.`

It never shows at the start of a duel or on attack turns: the dig lands on hesitation, not on playing. The sprite is one more Gemini sheet (see Art and animation), drawn translucent by the canvas; until it lands the grey box draws a pale placeholder.

The cost is built into the rules the game already has, which is why the buddy needs none of its own: a counter after the bar is half full earns no momentum, so a player who waits for the whisper gives momentum up and never drops the mic; the fallback still fires at the end, so waiting out the whole whisper loses the exchange; and a duel played with the buddy earns no flawless star. The result card names the run's mode. Everything else (collecting, the scale, the boss) is unchanged, and the reducer knows nothing about the buddy: it is presentation in the duel screen plus one flag on the duel setup for the star.

It is there for players who are here for the jokes rather than the memory test and for first runs; with the ghost the game is a quiz with a safety net, and the stars are for playing without one.

### First duel

The first duel is the tutorial whoever walks in: four options from any pool, no silence bar, and on the first exchange the dialogue box shows the hint `Pick the reply that answers it`. Nothing else explains the game; the title screen's three lines and the bars do the rest.

### Difficulty knobs

All in data: the slot table (menu size, decoy source, silence bar on or off and foreign-pool accuracy per slot), each character's borrowed pairs, the draw of two per role, hand size, the starting pool, momentum steps, mic drop value, and the boss mapping. Nothing is hard-coded. The numbers below are the ones the simulation settled on for v1; the sandbox and real play move them.

Rules behind the table:

- The player meets their own role first, then the two foreign ones. The own pool is known from the start, so the first two duels are on home ground: easy menus, no silence bar, the mechanics learnt on lines the player can already answer. The opponent knows that pool perfectly too, so attacks only land once fatigue sets in, which is the first lesson in how the scale moves. Each foreign pool then comes as a pair of colleagues: the first is lenient (obvious decoys, that duel collects the pool), the second strict. Tier belongs to the slot in the run, so whoever of the last foreign role shows up is the tier 3 fight.
- Each role has a distinct silhouette: the clothes and the head shape do the work (the hand-held items that were meant to help were dropped: pasted into the hand they looked wrong). The two looks of a role differ in everything: face, hair, skin, build, age, clothes. They are two people, not a palette swap, so each is its own model sheet. See Art and animation.
- The nine colleagues are drawn from these six sheets (which sheet each uses is in the cast table), so the cast is as mixed as the players. Two colleagues on one sheet are twins, so the run draw never shows both, nor the one on the player's own sheet; a third sheet per role would give every colleague a face of its own and the run its seventh duel back.
- Names are placeholders and only appear in the title screen and the save; duellists never use them in dialogue so lines stay role-neutral.
- Role choice is stored with the save. Switching role starts a new run; the old run is kept under its role so the player can finish all three (that is the completionist hook: one line of credits per finished role, three result cards, three full glossaries).

Start screen: the first thing a visitor sees is the logo, not a menu. The corner office at night fills the whole stage: its picture (the room is drawn smaller inside the 480 by 270 render, padded with a flat dark) scaled to cover the stage, a little cut off at the sides and smooth rather than on the art's pixels, dimmed only a little (15 percent; at 60 percent, and only in the band, the room was too dark to read), with the logo (the shove with a coffee cup for a U, prompt C of the prompt pack, cut on its pixel grid and keyed by `sheet.mjs` into `sheets/logo.png`, 226 by 77 native pixels, shown a quarter larger at 282 by 96 stage pixels) and `Press Enter to play` under it, centred on the stage, in Fira Code, blinking at 1 Hz (half a second on, half off, the arcade "insert coin" rhythm; steady under reduced motion), and the usual line small along the bottom, in the body text colour so it reads over the room: `All characters are fictional. Any resemblance to actual persons or teams is coincidental.` The cast is nine office archetypes; the line says so before anyone asks. Any key or a tap moves on: the logo slides up and a small window under it holds the menu (`Start`, `Mode`, `How to play`, and `Reset`, which only shows once there is a save; it asks in a confirmation dialog, then drops every run and the glossary and keeps the mode), so the title screen is the start screen with its menu open, not a second screen. The mode is a setting in the list, `Mode ◂ Normal ▸`: left and right switch it, stopping at the ends (the arrow at an end dimmed), Enter or a tap goes round; the arrows beside it say so, so the legend stays `↑↓ choose · Enter confirm`. The menu window used to span the screen with a blurb for the mode beside it; the blurb did not help and is gone. `How to play` in the menu shows how the game works (once shown on a first visit before the menu, which was a lot of text in the way; behind the menu item is enough): five numbered lines at the full text size in a window centred over the dimmed stage (the exchange, the scale, momentum and the mic drop, the collecting, and what the modes change: the ghost and no timer on easy, the bar from the second colleague on normal, less time, longer menus and a double score on hard; once the menu lost its mode blurb this was the only place to say it), `Enter continue`; Enter, Escape or a tap goes back, the cursor still on the item. The logo's rules follow what pixel-art logo guides agree on: shape first, so it reads at thumbnail size and in one flat colour; a thick ink outline and a hard two-pixel drop shadow for lift; one distinctive element rather than stacked effects, here the letters of BACK shoved out of line as if pushed, and a speech-bubble tail off the K, since the game is a duel of lines. The wordmark is also the lab index card's picture and, over the office scene, the link preview. Attract mode: twenty seconds of nothing on the title (no key, no tap) and the game plays a demo, the way an arcade cabinet draws people over: a real duel from a random seed (`demoFor`: a role and a look at random against a colleague of another role on another sheet, tier 2, no ghost, no silence bar, both pools known), the AI on both sides with the player's menus picked after a pause and the right answer seven times in ten, stamped like an arcade cabinet's demo (`DemoLabel`: `DEMO` and `Press Enter to play` one size, bold, slanted (the browser's oblique of Fira Code, which has no italic), in gold with a hard one-pixel ink outline and a drop like the logo's, blinking together, centred at the duellists' chests under their faces; a big `DEMO` over a smaller call was too much in the face; a see-through `DEMO` across the whole band came first and vanished over the light walls, then a stripe across the room, dark and still, then pale with a ticker running along it; the inventory plays the demo live, without the twenty seconds), the window's legend reading `Any key back to the title`; any key or tap returns, and so does the end of the duel after a few seconds. Nothing of it is saved.

Selection screen: the six looks as a grid of faces over the dimmed corner office, the way a fighting game shows its roster: a column per role, a row per look, each tile the head square `features.mjs` found in the idle cell at twice the stage's pixels, the chosen one in a thick gold frame that blinks four times a second, the others dimmed. The chosen look stands beside the grid, breathing, with its name under it and `in progress` or `finished` when a run exists; the role's one-line blurb (`Has a roadmap and is not afraid to use it.` and so on, see Lines) sits in the quiet window below with the legend `←→ role · ↑↓ look · Enter play`. One cursor moves over the grid: left/right across the columns, up/down between the rows, both wrapping, nothing remembered per role; a tap on a face picks it, Enter or a second tap plays.

### The run

A run is four slots: three colleagues and the Stakeholder. Each run draws one of the three characters per role at random, seeded per run, the own role first and the two foreign roles in random order. No face shows twice: the draw skips any character on the player's own sheet or on a sheet the run already has (`drawRun`, `bossIndex`). The six characters not drawn are out of office this run and rotate in on the next one, which is the discovery a short run lives on: nine colleagues for three chairs. The slot table applies by position:

| Slot | Role in the run     | Tier | Menu | Decoys from | Silence bar | Outside its repertoire |
| ---- | ------------------- | ---- | ---- | ----------- | ----------- | ---------------------- |
| 1    | Own role            | 1    | 4    | any pool    | off         | 60 %                   |
| 2    | First foreign role  | 2    | 5    | any pool    | on          | 70 %                   |
| 3    | Second foreign role | 3    | 6    | any pool    | on          | 85 %                   |
| 4    | The Stakeholder     | Boss | 6    | all pools   | on          | 100 %                  |

"Decoys from" is where the wrong counters in the player's defence menu are drawn from, within the deck: any pool means the wrong answers are obviously from another conversation, same pool means they all sound like plausible replies. Every slot keeps the obvious decoys, because with one encounter per role the player meets each foreign pool for the first time in its duel; the third slot is harder through the bar and the opponent's accuracy, not through tricky decoys. ("Same pool" and "two pools" stay in the data as steps for a later ramp.) A mic drop sets the menu to eight whatever the slot. "Outside its repertoire" is the AI's accuracy on phrases it has not got; its repertoire it answers every time.

With these numbers the simulation's modelled player (recalls a collected pair 90 % of the time) wins a first attempt at 92, 64, 57 and 93 % across the slots, a retry at 90 % and up, and plays five duels per run; it reaches the boss with 17 to 18 of the deck's 24 pairs. The own-role duel is the warm-up, the two foreign ones are the dips, and the boss moves with how well the player knows the lines: it never attacks wrongly, so the finale is a memory test of the deck, which is why hard mode exists.

The ramp is fixed and the faces and lines are random, which is the point: the first duel is always the tutorial whoever walks in, and the run never opens with a silence bar. A run is about five minutes, and the next one has other colleagues and other lines.

Between duels: a single roster screen over the darkened corner office, built from the quiet windows like the duel: a title (`Your run as Dev`) with a one-line explainer, the slots as a list in run order in a window with the legend along its bottom (`↑↓ choose · Enter play · Tab glossary 8/24 · Esc back`), the name and a tick when beaten; a second mark (a star) when beaten flawless, that is without losing an exchange and without the buddy. The out-of-office characters are listed in a narrow window beside the list under `Out of office`, so the player sees the cast is bigger than the run. The next unbeaten slot is selected, beaten ones can be replayed for the star or to collect what they throw, the Stakeholder is greyed until all six ticks are there. Enter or tap starts the duel; Escape returns to the title. Tab (or the `Glossary` link in the title row) shows the glossary: three windows, one per pool, every pair the player knows with its counter under it and a dashed line for each one still to collect.

The glossary: a second tab on the roster (Tab or a tap on `Glossary 11/48` in the corner), three columns of sixteen slots, one per pool, across every run the save has seen. A collected pair shows phrase and counter; an uncollected one shows a blank line in its pool's column. The pairs in play this run that the player has not met yet are among the blanks, so the column still says which role has lines left to hear. The count goes on the result card and is the reason to play again: a run can fill at most 24 of the 48.

### The Stakeholder

- Only reachable after the three colleagues are beaten (the save tracks a flag per slot).
- Never gives up the initiative. The whole duel is a barrage of the six boss phrases, one after the other, in random order, no regular phrases; past six, the barrage goes round again without repeating the last one.
- The player has to push the scale four steps, the Stakeholder only three (two in hard mode). Every counter pushes one, every miss pushes back one.
- Every boss phrase is answered by one regular counter. The mapping is fixed in data and the same for every role. The defence menu's six options draw decoys from all three pools, so the player has to recognise the reply in a new context (the Monkey Island Sword Master trick: known counters, new phrases).
- Momentum is the only way to get a word in. With a full bar the player interrupts: they throw one phrase from a hand of four, the Stakeholder doesn't look up, says `Let's take this offline.`, and the player fills two segments. Two fast counters then the interrupt is the flawless boss win in three exchanges, and that bark is the reward for the hardest fight.
- On loss, the boss bark names the pool of the counter the player last got wrong (`Have you talked to engineering about this?` when it was a Dev line, and so on). Hints are the only help, and this one is a pointer: that pool's column in the glossary has the blanks, and that role's slots are a tap away on the roster.
- The boss can test a pair the player never heard; the counter is in the menu like anywhere else, so it is a guess, not a dead end. Losing to it once and knowing why is the intended experience. The boss is not locked behind a full glossary; that would turn the replay from a choice into homework.

### The ending

No twist about who the Stakeholder is. "The stakeholder was the CEO" is what every reader assumes from the suit and the phone, so it would be a confirmation, not a reveal, and it would turn a joke about meetings into one about hierarchy, which breaks the tone rule above. The reveal is about the player instead:

- **The losing line is keyed to the player's role.** The Stakeholder used to be one of you: `Fine. Put it in the next steering deck. I used to write those.` for a PM, `… I used to review those.` for a Dev, `… I used to draw those.` for a Designer. Three variants of one bark, no art, and it lands because everyone has met the ex-developer in the corner office. The joke is that you were playing against your own future.
- **The phone**, if it reads at native size (decided in the polish package): when the Stakeholder finally looks up after the loss, the glowing phone screen on the desk, already a living detail of the scene, shows the Pushback title screen for one beat. They were playing it the whole time. One small sprite swap in the loop script, self-referential, and keeps "merely busy".

### AI behaviour

On attack: pick a random phrase from the opponent's pool, no repeats within a duel until all are used, then any. The pick order depends on the attempt: on a first attempt at a slot, and on a replay of a beaten slot, uncollected pairs first (collecting); on a retry after a loss, alternate collected and uncollected, collected first (the earned retry). Within each group the pick is random. With full momentum, slots at tier 1 and 2 drop the mic at once, tier 3 holds it until the hit would end the duel; a mic drop throws one of the opponent's role's three closers, unthrown ones first. On defence: own pool always right; foreign pools right with the slot's accuracy, otherwise a random wrong counter from the phrase's pool. The Stakeholder never guesses and never attacks with a mic drop (every boss phrase is already worth a miss).

## Result card

After the boss, win or lose, the run's end as an RPG status screen: one quiet window filling the stage, the player's face (the head square at three times its pixels, framed) with the name and role on the left, the result in gold (`Stakeholder beaten` or `Taken offline`) and the run's numbers on the right as a `Ledger` (duels, flawless, exchanges, mic drops, each with a dot leader from the label to the value, the way arcade score tables line up), and under a rule the glossary by pool, a `Meter` of sixteen boxes per pool lit as far as it is collected, the count and the counters missed, so the gaps show. The mode is not on it. `Copy result` puts the text card on the clipboard as plain text (no analytics needed; this is the Wordle trick). The roster shows it again for a finished run. The numbers come from `resultModel`, the text from `resultCardText`, both in `save.ts`; the inventory shows the screen for any role and look.

```
Pushback · Dev
Stakeholder beaten · 10 duels · 5 flawless · normal mode
Score 18,240
Exchanges 31–9 · Mic drops 6 · Glossary 24/48
Missed counters: PM 3 · Dev 1 · Designer 5
sebald.me/lab/pushback
```

Everything on it comes from the stats in the save (see Tech).

The score is one number to beat on a replay and to share, next to the ticks and stars on the roster, which stay: a tick says beaten, a star says beaten without losing an exchange and without the ghost, and the number says how well. A won duel scores (`SCORE`, `duelScore`):

| Points    | For                                                                                                |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 100       | every exchange won                                                                                 |
| up to 100 | every right counter, by how much of the menu's reading time was left (`speedPoints`, timer or not) |
| 200       | every mic drop                                                                                     |
| 500       | the win                                                                                            |
| 500       | a flawless win                                                                                     |
| 1000      | the Stakeholder                                                                                    |

times the mode's factor (easy 1, normal 1.5, hard 2), on tens; a lost duel scores nothing. The reading time for the speed points is the median reader's for the whole menu in every mode, so speed counts the same with or without a bar, and the duel counts the time on every defence (`answerMs`). A run keeps the best win of each colleague (`beaten[i].score`), and its score is their sum (`runScore`), so a replay can only raise it. The result screen shows it large under the result, with the mode and its factor. A lost boss fight says `Stakeholder took it offline` instead of `beaten` on the card, `Taken offline` on the screen.

## Art and animation

Nothing is drawn by hand. Gemini generates every image (free tier), `snap.mjs` pulls it onto the pixel grid, scripts in the repo do the compositing, palette work and animation, and Claude Code reviews and repairs the pixels. Sprites stay at the pixel size Gemini draws them. Gemini's 2752 by 1536 renders are 240 columns of 11.465 px, so `snap.mjs` fits a fractional pitch and phase (a whole-pixel grid drifted a cell every two cells and smeared every face right of the first) and the figures come out 76 to 80 px tall (the first Dev sheet, a 1376 px render, came out at 76 and was redrawn); sheets are cut from the Gemini originals in the ignored `sources/originals/`, not the recompressed copies; the height range for sheets is 70 to 85 px on a 72 by 112 cell, and roles must not differ in height for no reason (a sheet outside the range is rerolled, not scaled); two exceptions by decision, Bo and Kim, whose first roll came at a finer pitch (94 and 95 px) and was kept because a broad man and a tall woman a head above the rest read as builds and give the cast body types, so the test's ceiling is 96. Downscaling to 48 px lost the face and the headphones, so the stage is 480 by 270; a figure is then a bit under a third of the screen height, the classic adventure-game proportion. Same palette discipline as the headers (3 to 4 shades per material, dark outline on the silhouette only), same pixel size as the scene.

Why Gemini and not a sprite tool: the AI sprite services (PixelLab and similar) are paid, and the Claude Code pixel plugins only script Aseprite, which is paid or a self-compile; neither draws a better figure than a reference-steered Gemini prompt. Gemini holds a character steady inside one image, so every character is generated as a single model sheet, not frame by frame.

The prompts for every image are in `docs/plans/pushback-prompts.md`: fourteen generations before rerolls (seven model sheets, the ghost, four scenes, one prop sheet, one head sheet), each with the references to attach, the file name under `sources/` and what to reroll on.

Pipeline per character:

1. Prompt Gemini for one model sheet: the figure six times in a row (idle, talk-open, taunt, flinch, win, lose), same character, same scale, front three-quarter view, plain dark background, a header attached as the style reference, the pixel-header template with `No people` removed and `Six copies of the same figure side by side, identical in size and colours, each in a different pose` added. Reroll until the six are the same person. PM, Designer and Stakeholder use the same prompt with the Dev sheet attached as a second reference, so scale and pixel size match.
2. `snap.mjs` at the sheet's detected grid (6 px on the Dev render), nothing forced; all six poses share one grid because they come from one image.
3. `sheet.mjs` keys out the flat background (a flood from the border, plus the soft shadow under the feet), finds the figures as the six largest connected groups (so a hand reaching into the next figure's columns still belongs to its own), stands each one on the bottom row of a 72 by 112 cell, locks the palette to 32 colours by merging the closest shades (the sharp that ships with Next has no quantiser) and writes the sheet, its cell boxes as JSON and a 4x contact print. Rectangles in `patches.json` are painted over a cell after the cut, for a brand mark not worth a reroll (the energy drink's claw became two bands).
4. Claude Code reviews the contact print (it can view the PNG) against the checklist in the `pixel-sprite` skill: same height across poses, no stray single pixels, mouth and prop readable, palette within the scene. It fixes small faults in code (`sheet.mjs` has `setPx`-style helpers as in `loop.mjs`) and sends larger faults back to step 1 with a follow-up prompt in the same Gemini chat (`Keep this exactly, but …`).

Opponents are not new drawings: two model sheets per role (the two looks the player can pick) plus one for the Stakeholder, so seven Gemini sheets in total, and each colleague is drawn from one of its role's two sheets as it is. The browser builds each sprite once from its sheet (`src/ui/lab/pushback/sheets.ts`). Sprites are never recoloured in code: hue-based tints caught skin and hair next to the garment and left pixels see-through, so they were taken out; and nothing is pasted on: the hand-held items from a Gemini item sheet looked wrong in the hand, so they were dropped with the item sheet. A colleague that needs a look of its own gets a sheet of its own; until then the run draw keeps the twins apart (see The run). The head sheet (hood up, beard) was not generated. The onboarding buddy's ghost is an eighth sheet: three cells (hover, hover, point), no legs, keyed like the others and drawn at 0.7 opacity. Gemini drew it a quarter taller than the people however it was asked, so it is the one sheet that is scaled (nearest neighbour to 0.78): a pale, soft figure with no face detail to lose. The Gemini originals are kept in the repo as `src/assets/lab/pushback/sources/<id>.jpg`; files under `src/assets` only ship when imported, so nothing excludes them from the build, they are simply never imported. They are the reference image for every later prompt, and a snapped PNG works worse as one.

If a sheet comes back with only four usable poses, the fallback is flinch for lose and taunt for win; the spec allows it and the data marks which cells exist. The right-hand duellist is the sheet mirrored; props with text are avoided in the prompts for that reason. The Stakeholder never leaves idle or talk during the barrage; the interrupt is the one time they flinch, and the lose pose is the one time they look up.

Data shapes (the spec's tables map 1:1 onto these):

```typescript
type Role = 'pm' | 'dev' | 'designer';

type Pair = { id: string; role: Role; phrase: string; counter: string };
type BossPair = { id: string; phrase: string; answeredBy: Pair['id'] };

type Tier = 1 | 2 | 3 | 'boss';

type Character = {
  id: string;
  name: string;
  role: Role | 'boss';
  sheet: SheetId;
  scene: 'floor' | 'meeting' | 'studio' | 'office';
  intro: string;
};

type Slot = {
  tier: Tier;
  accuracy: number; // on foreign pools; own pool is always 1
  menu: number; // counter options on defence
  decoys: 'any' | 'two' | 'same' | 'all';
  silence: boolean; // the bar runs; its length follows the menu's text
};

type Run = {
  seed: number;
  slots: { character: Character['id']; slot: Slot }[]; // 6 colleagues + the boss
};

type Rules = {
  reach: number; // 3: steps of the scale to win
  bossReach: number; // 4
  bossMisses: number; // 3 (hard 2)
  momentum: number; // 2
  momentumLoss: 'all' | number; // 'all'
  micDrop: { worth: number; menu: number; silence: number }; // 2, 8, 0.75
  reading: { wordsPerSecond: number; share: number }; // 4, 1.1 (hard 0.7)
  hand: number; // 4
  fatigue: number; // 0.1 (hard 0.07)
  menuExtra: number; // 0 (hard 2)
  timer: boolean; // the silence bar runs (easy off)
  score: number; // the mode's factor on the score: easy 1, normal 1.5, hard 2
};

type RunStats = {
  exchangesWon: number;
  exchangesLost: number;
  micDrops: number;
  missesByPool: Record<Role, number>;
  speed: number; // speed points of the right counters
};

type Save = {
  version: 3; // 2 is read too, its normal as easy
  mode: 'easy' | 'normal' | 'hard'; // the mode switch on the title screen
  glossary: Pair['id'][]; // every pair ever collected, across runs
  runs: Partial<
    Record<
      Role,
      {
        run: Run; // the draw, so a reload keeps the same cast
        beaten: Record<number, { flawless: boolean; score: number }>; // by slot index, the best win's score
        mode?: 'easy' | 'normal' | 'hard'; // the last duel's, for the result card
        collected: Pair['id'][]; // starts as the role's own pool
        finished: boolean;
        stats: RunStats;
      }
    >
  >;
};
```

Duel state machine (one reducer, screens derived from `phase`):

1. `intro`: opponent bark, then `attack` with the opponent holding the initiative (the challenger always opens). The duel starts with an `attempt` of `first`, `retry` (the slot was lost before and is not beaten) or `replay` (the slot is beaten), which sets the AI's pick order.
2. `attack`: attacker's line types out. Player attacker picks from the hand of four or, with full momentum, the closer offered under it (`closer` in the state; throwing it is the mic drop); AI picks per AI behaviour.
3. `defend`: counter menu (player, with the silence bar sending `tick` actions and a `timeout` that plays the fallback) or AI pick. Resolves to `resolve`.
4. `resolve`: taunt/flinch poses, buy-in segment fills, momentum updates; on a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat. A pair the player just defended against is added to `collected`. Initiative to the winner. Full buy-in → `end`, else `attack`.
5. `end`: win/lose poses and barks (the boss's loss bark keyed to the pool of the last missed counter), save written with the stats, then back to the roster or, after the boss, the result card and the credits line.

The boss duel runs the same reducer with `buyIn: 4`, the initiative pinned to the boss, a miss counter that ends the duel at three, and the interrupt as the one `attack` phase the player gets, entered from `resolve` when momentum is full.

Rendering loop: a single `requestAnimationFrame` loop at 8 fps steps (125 ms, matching the scene loops) draws scene frame, left sprite, right sprite and chrome into the 480 by 270 canvas. Dialogue and menus are real DOM (text and buttons overlaid on the canvas, styled to match) so they are accessible and tappable; the canvas is decorative and gets `aria-hidden`, the current line is also written to a visually hidden live region, and the bars have text equivalents there (`Buy-in 2 of 3, momentum full`).

Input: arrow keys or W/S to move in menus, Enter or Space to confirm and to skip typing, Escape to pause. Touch: tap a menu item, tap the stage to skip typing. The mic drop needs no key of its own: it is the gold line in the hand. The whole thing works with a screen reader through the DOM menus and the live region, with the silence bar off.

Reduced motion: scenes show the poster, breathing and talk cycle are off, typing is instant. Same rule as the headers.

Tests: `duel.ts` and `ai.ts` under the Node test runner (buy-in and initiative, momentum gains and resets, mic drop worth and cost, the closer on offer at full momentum only and the three in turn, the opponent's mic drop a closer of its role answered in the menu, the hand draws from collected pool phrases only and never repeats one within a duel, a run starts with exactly the role's own pool collected and a defended pair is collected win or lose, the AI throws uncollected pairs first on a first attempt and a replay and alternates from a collected pair on a retry, a run draws two distinct characters per role in the role order and the slot table applies by position, decoy rules and menu size per slot, own-pool answers are always right, the silence timeout plays the fallback as a miss, the boss barrage ends on the sixth phrase under every outcome, the interrupt fires only at full momentum, boss mapping completeness: every boss phrase has exactly one answering pair that exists; every line under 70 characters; the result card text matches the stats). `sheets.test.ts` validates the committed sheets. One Playwright VRT screenshot of the title screen and one of a frozen duel frame, added to the existing visual suite (ask before running VRT, per `AGENTS.md`).

### Components

The screens are built from pieces that work on their own, the way the site's components do, so each can be shown and tuned in the inventory and reused later, and nothing is styled twice. Three foundations under all of them: `PALETTE` (`palette.ts`, every colour of the chrome and the letters in one list, which the canvas reads as hex and the DOM as CSS variables the stage sets on its root, `text-(--pb-muted)`, `bg-(--pb-fill)`; the sprites and the rooms keep their own colours), the letters by role (`text.tsx`: `Heading`, `Muted` for prompts, blurbs and hints, `Loud` for the gold line blinking at the arcade rhythm, with `--blink-low` for how far it goes, and `Dialogue` for a line with its speaker's mark), and `px()` from the stage for a length in stage pixels. A screen never writes a hex colour or its own muted paragraph; it reaches for these. The pieces: `Character` (one duellist from a sheet: the pose, mirrored or not, typing, a mic drop; a 72 by 112 canvas at the stage's device pixels on its own clock, breathing when idle), `Ghost` (hovering, pointing, in and out of its black hole), `Hud` (the names, the momentum pips and the shared scale with its marker, every number a prop), `Stage` (the scaled frame, which provides its scale to everything inside through `useStage`, and `Pixels`, the bare provider of the pixel unit for pieces shown outside a stage), `Frame` (the room band with the loop, the pieces standing in it, and the chrome canvas), `Window` (the quiet window as DOM, pixel for pixel the drawn one: ink, frame, inner line, fill, the dithered gradient at its top and bottom edges, notched corners; it grows with its content and takes a `legend`), `Legend` (the control hints along the window's bottom edge, only what works right now, in key or touch wording by the last input), `Dialogue` (a line with its speaker's mark), `Picker` (the list the player moves through, with an `accent` item, the loud one, for the closer on offer, `dim` for what the ghost fades and `disabled` for what the keys skip); given less room than its items need, a hard-mode mic drop in a short window, the list scrolls, keeps the current item in view and shows a pixel rail beside it, an arrow lit at each end where there is more and a track with where the list is, and the duel caps its window at the bars (`LAYOUT.hud`) so it grows over the room but never over them, `Burst` (the mic-drop starburst from its sheet, pulsing or popped) and the screens. The pause panel and the result card are the same `Window`.

### Inventory

Tests are enough for the rules and not enough for anything you look at. The reducer, the AI, the draw and the save are pure and the tests above pin them down; what a test cannot tell is whether a 74-pixel sprite reads at 2x, whether six seconds of silence is tense or cruel, whether the `Mic drop!` label lands on the right frame, or whether a palette swap is the wrong shade. Playing through to the slot in question to find out is a tuning loop measured in minutes, so the game gets an inventory, like the site's `/inventory`: every piece on its own with controls. Not a second site: a dev-only route in the same app, `/lab/pushback/inventory`, which calls `notFound()` in production. Same bundler, Tailwind, fonts, asset imports and fast refresh as the game, no parallel config for the one thing that makes the pixels right (integer scaling and the asset pipeline). No Storybook, no Vite page.

Its sections, the style guide first, so a piece is adjusted where it is shown:

- **Palette.** Every colour with its name, its hex and its CSS variable, grouped by what it is for.
- **Text.** The letters by role at the stage's scale on the window's fill: headings, body, a line to pick, muted, the loud one (blinking to half, and the title's full blink), the dialogue marks, the legend.
- **Picker.** The list on its own: the cursor, the loud item, items the ghost has dimmed, a disabled one.
- **Window.** The quiet window with a line typing, with the phrase (at full strength: it is what the player reads to pick), the prompt with room on both sides, the picker and the legend; with the hand and a closer on offer in gold; and once more with ten options in a window as short as a cropped stage allows, so the rail shows.
- **Character.** One duellist at a time: who, the pose, mirrored, typing, a mic drop, still, 1x to 6x; idle by default so the breathing shows. **Cast** shows everyone idle. The contact print from `sheet.mjs` is the still version of this; the playground is where motion and swaps get checked.
- **Ghost.** Hovering or pointing, and the black hole on demand.
- **Bars.** The names, the pips and the scale over a flat band, every number on a slider, the crop too.
- **Demo.** The attract-mode duel playing right away, a new one on a press.
- **Mic drop.** The burst's three cells and the pulse.
- **Scene viewer.** Each scene with its loop and its poster, at the same scales, with the stand positions and the clear band drawn as outlines so a new render can be checked against the composition rule, and two idle figures on the stands (any of the looks or the cast) as the yardstick for the furniture, with a slider that scales the room about the stands' feet while the figures stay, to find the factor the render should be regenerated or resampled to: a desk reaches a hip, a chair a knee, a door a head and a half. The floor scene's desk islands fail that test and are the first scene to regenerate. After three rounds the desks reached the hip and the chairs, tables and doors still stood a fifth to a half too tall, so the rooms are written a fifth smaller than drawn (`shrink.mjs`, a smooth downscale about the floor line with every pixel snapped back to the loop's palette, so the one-pixel outlines keep a tone where a nearest-neighbour shrink would drop every fifth one); the loop scripts and `native.png` stay in the render's pixels.
- **Duel sandbox.** Pick character and slot, set a seed, jump to any phase, fill momentum, force the barrage, an override for the silence bar's share of the reading time, a reduced-motion toggle. Because the reducer is pure, "jump to phase" is constructing a state and handing it to the same stage component the game uses, so the sandbox exercises the real thing, not a copy.

One script beside it, `simulate.ts`, run with the same tsx loader as the tests: a modelled player (answers collected pairs with a given accuracy, guesses the rest at one over the menu size, respects the silence bar with a given speed) plays full runs against the slot table, a thousand seeded runs per setting, and prints win rate per slot on first attempts and on retries (a retry must win more often than a first attempt, or the loop is not earned), average duels to beat the boss, and glossary size when the boss is reached. That is how the 60/75/90, the silence seconds and the boss's four segments get set with numbers before anyone plays, and it is the check that the boss is a wall for a player who knows nothing and not for one who collected the room.

The workbench grows with the packages: the simulation in package 1 (it only needs the rules), the sandbox with the grey box in package 3, the sprite and scene viewers as the assets land in 4 and 5.
