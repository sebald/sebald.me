# Pushback: Game Spec

Oct 26, 2026 · @Sebastian · revised Oct 27

## Overview

Pushback is a small browser game on sebald.me: a duel of phrases and counters between the roles of a product team, in the pixel-art style of the note headers. The player picks a role (PM, Dev or Designer), works through a gauntlet of colleagues and faces the Stakeholder as the final boss. Each exchange is won by picking the right counter to the phrase just thrown, or by throwing a phrase the opponent cannot answer; the duel is won by filling your buy-in bar first.

The three rules on the title screen: Counter the phrase. Fill your buy-in. Full momentum lets you drop the mic.

Scope for v1:

- One route, `/lab/pushback`, no backend, no accounts; progress lives in `localStorage`.
- Three playable roles, nine regular opponents (three per role), one boss.
- 76 lines of dialogue (24 phrase/counter pairs, 6 boss phrases, 22 barks and fallbacks), all original text. The mechanic is borrowed from the Monkey Island insult sword fighting, the words are not, and the collecting part of that mechanic is left out: this is a mini game, not an adventure, so every phrase and counter is in play from the start. The two bars are borrowed from fighting games and that is where the borrowing stops.
- Four backgrounds made with the existing `pixel-header` pipeline, character sprites generated by Gemini at their native pixel size.
- Keyboard and touch playable, 480 by 270 stage scaled by integers to the viewport.
- A result card after the boss that can be copied as text.

Out of scope for v1: sound (the repo has an audio folder, but music is a v2 question), a map or overworld, multiplayer, localisation (English only, like the blog), and any analytics beyond the existing Vercel counter. Role passives and a daily seed are listed under After v1.

Tone: dry and affectionate. Every role gets roasted and every role gets to roast back. Nobody is the villain, not even the Stakeholder, who is merely very busy.

## Core mechanic

A duel is a sequence of exchanges. The attacker throws a phrase, the defender picks a counter from a short menu; the right counter wins the exchange and swaps the initiative, a miss keeps it with the attacker. Every won exchange fills one segment of the winner's buy-in bar. Full buy-in wins the duel.

### The two bars

Each duellist has two bars above their head, drawn as native-size chrome:

- **Buy-in**, three segments (four against the boss). One segment per won exchange, two for a landed mic drop. Full bar wins. It only fills; nothing drains. This is the score, not health: both players build towards something.
- **Momentum**, three steps. Playing well fills it, losing an exchange empties it. Full momentum unlocks the mic drop.

| Event                                                   | Momentum   |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Counter before the silence bar is half full             | +1         |
| Counter after that (or with the silence bar off: any counter) | +1 only when the bar is off, else 0 |
| Landed phrase (the opponent missed)                     | +1         |
| Lost exchange (missed counter or a countered phrase)    | reset to 0 |

### The mic drop

With full momentum and the initiative, the attacker can drop the mic: the next phrase is worth two buy-in segments instead of one. It costs the whole momentum bar and raises the stakes for the defender: the counter menu grows to eight and the silence bar runs at half time. The opponent can drop the mic too, telegraphed by the taunt pose and a `Mic drop!` label over the dialogue box, so a full enemy momentum bar is a visible threat.

For the AI as defender the menu size makes no difference, so a player's mic drop against it is simply worth two; the decision it leaves the player is which phrase to spend it on (see Who knows what: a mic drop with a phrase from the opponent's own pool is a wasted bar). Momentum resets on any lost exchange, so holding a full bar for the perfect moment is the risk and spending it now is the safe play.

### The exchange, step by step

1. Attacker steps forward, line types out at 30 characters per second (tap or Space to complete instantly).
2. Defender gets a menu of counters in random order: the one that answers the phrase plus decoys. Menu size and decoy source depend on the opponent's tier (see Opponents): four options from any pool against the Intern, six from the same pool against a tier 3. From tier 2 on the silence bar runs under the menu (see below).
3. Match: the counter wins, the attacker flinches, the defender gets the initiative and fills a segment. Miss: the defender flinches, the attacker keeps the initiative and fills a segment (two on a mic drop). On a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat before the flinch, so a lost exchange still teaches the pair.
4. Full buy-in ends the duel with win and lose poses and one bark each.

### Throwing a phrase: the hand

When the player has the initiative they do not get all 24 phrases; they get a hand of four, drawn from the phrases not yet thrown in this duel, seeded per duel. A thrown phrase is spent for the duel and the hand refills from the rest on the next attack turn. Four fit on a phone, no two duels are the same, and the choice is real because of the next rule. 24 phrases over at most five regular exchanges never run out.

### Who knows what

An opponent knows its own pool perfectly and answers phrases from the other two pools correctly with its tier's accuracy (60, 75 or 90 %). The Intern can answer any PM line but struggles with a Dev line; the Architect answers Dev lines every time. So on attack the player works out which pool the opponent is weak against and throws from that pool, and a hand with no foreign phrase is a bad hand to drop the mic from. The Stakeholder answers everything.

### The silence bar

From tier 2 on the defender has a few seconds to answer. A thin bar under the menu fills while the player hesitates; when it is full the game answers for them with the fallback line `Uh… that's a good point.`, which counts as a miss. Memorised pairs still have to come out under pressure, which is what keeps tier 3 and the boss hard once the player knows the lines. Seconds per tier are in data (starting values: 8 s at tier 2, 6 s at tier 3, 5 s against the boss, halved on a mic drop) and get tuned in the grey box.

The bar is off at tier 1, off by default under `prefers-reduced-motion` and switchable on the title screen for screen reader users and anyone who wants a quiz instead of a duel. With the bar off every correct counter counts as fast for momentum.

### First duel

The Intern is the tutorial: four options from any pool, no silence bar, and on the first exchange the dialogue box shows the hint `Pick the reply that answers it`. Nothing else explains the game; the title screen's three lines and the bars do the rest.

### Difficulty knobs

All in data: menu size, decoy source, silence seconds and which pairs an opponent throws per tier, the AI's accuracy on foreign pools, hand size, momentum steps, mic drop value, and the boss mapping. Nothing is hard-coded.

## Player characters

The player picks one of three roles on the title screen. The pick changes the sprite, the order of opponents and the boss's framing; the mechanic and the lines are the same for all three.

| Role     | Working name | Look                                           | Prop               | Opponent order                              |
| -------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| PM       | Jo           | Lanyard, rolled sleeves, notebook under the arm | Sticky-note stack  | Dev team, then Designers, then PMs, then boss |
| Dev      | Sam          | Hoodie, headphones around the neck             | Laptop with stickers | Designers, then PMs, then Devs, then boss  |
| Designer | Ines         | Turtleneck, round glasses                      | Tablet with stylus | PMs, then Devs, then Designers, then boss   |

Rules behind the table:

- The player always meets their own role last before the boss. Fighting your own kind is the hardest tier because those lines are the ones you find funniest and least fair, and because your own pool is the one the opponent knows perfectly, so your attacks have to come from elsewhere.
- Each role has a distinct silhouette: the prop and the head shape do most of the work, colours do the rest. See Art and animation for the paper-doll layout.
- Names are placeholders and only appear in the title screen and the save; duellists never use them in dialogue so lines stay role-neutral.
- Role choice is stored with the save. Switching role starts a new run; the old run is kept under its role so the player can finish all three (that is the completionist hook: one line of credits per finished role, and three result cards).

Selection screen: three sprites in idle pose on a plain dark background, the active one lit, name and a one-line blurb below (`Has a roadmap and is not afraid to use it.` / `Will fix it in the next sprint. Which sprint is a detail.` / `Has opinions about your line height.`). Left/right or tap to pick, Enter or tap again to confirm.

## Opponents

Nine regular opponents, three per role, each a palette and prop swap of that role's base sprite, plus the Stakeholder. Tier sets which pairs the opponent throws, the size and plausibility of the player's defence menu, the silence bar, and how often the AI answers phrases from the other two pools.

| Opponent            | Role     | Tier | Throws       | Menu | Decoys from | Silence | Foreign pools | Distinguishing swap                 |
| ------------------- | -------- | ---- | ------------ | ---- | ----------- | ------- | ------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| The Intern          | PM       | 1    | 4 pairs      | 4    | any pool    | off     | 60 %          | Oversized lanyard, coffee           |
| The Roadmap Owner   | PM       | 2    | 5 pairs      | 5    | two pools   | 8 s     | 75 %          | Gantt printout                      |
| The Head of Product | PM       | 3    | 5 pairs      | 6    | same pool   | 6 s     | 90 %          | Blazer, no notebook                 |
| The Junior          | Dev      | 1    | 4 pairs      | 4    | any pool    | off     | 60 %          | Hoodie up, energy drink             |
| The Senior          | Dev      | 2    | 5 pairs      | 5    | two pools   | 8 s     | 75 %          | Mechanical keyboard under the arm   |
| The Architect       | Dev      | 3    | 5 pairs      | 6    | same pool   | 6 s     | 90 %          | Whiteboard marker, grey beard       |
| The Freelancer      | Designer | 1    | 4 pairs      | 4    | any pool    | off     | 60 %          | Tote bag                            |
| The Brand Designer  | Designer | 2    | 5 pairs      | 5    | two pools   | 8 s     | 75 %          | Pantone swatch                      |
| The Design Lead     | Designer | 3    | 5 pairs      | 6    | same pool   | 6 s     | 90 %          | Very small laptop                   |
| The Stakeholder     | Boss     | Boss | 6 boss pairs | 6    | all pools   | 5 s     | 100 %         | Suit, phone in hand, never looks up |

"Decoys from" is where the wrong counters in the player's defence menu are drawn from: any pool means the wrong answers are obviously from another conversation, same pool means they all sound like plausible replies. "Two pools" is the phrase's own pool plus one other, picked per exchange. A mic drop sets the menu to eight whatever the tier. "Foreign pools" is the AI's accuracy on phrases outside its own role; its own pool it answers every time.

How a run goes: the player's opponent order (see Player characters) is three tiers of a role, then the next role, then their own. Within a role the tiers are fixed (1, 2, 3). That is nine duels plus the boss; a run is 20 to 30 minutes.

Between duels: a single roster screen over the darkened corner office, the ten opponents as a vertical list in run order with their sprite, name and a tick when beaten; a second mark (a star) when beaten flawless, that is without losing an exchange. The next unbeaten one is selected, beaten ones can be replayed for the star, the Stakeholder is greyed until all nine ticks are there. Enter or tap starts the duel; Escape returns to the title.

### The Stakeholder

- Only reachable after all nine are beaten (the save tracks a flag per opponent).
- Never gives up the initiative. The Stakeholder's buy-in bar is full when the fight starts (the joke: they don't need yours), and the whole duel is a barrage of the six boss phrases, one after the other, in random order, no regular phrases.
- The player's buy-in bar has four segments. Every counter fills one; three misses lose. The maths closes on the sixth phrase: four right and two misses is a win, three misses is a loss, so the last phrase always decides when it gets that far.
- Every boss phrase is answered by one regular counter. The mapping is fixed in data and the same for every role. The defence menu's six options draw decoys from all three pools, so the player has to recognise the reply in a new context (the Monkey Island Sword Master trick: known counters, new phrases).
- Momentum is the only way to get a word in. With a full bar the player interrupts: they throw one phrase from a hand of four, the Stakeholder doesn't look up, says `Let's take this offline.`, and the player fills two segments. Three fast counters then the interrupt is the flawless boss win in four exchanges, and that bark is the reward for the hardest fight.
- On loss, the boss bark names the pool of the counter the player last got wrong (`Have you talked to engineering about this?` when it was a Dev line, and so on). Hints are the only help.

### AI behaviour

On attack: pick a random phrase from the opponent's `throws`, no repeats until all are used, then any. With full momentum, tiers 1 and 2 drop the mic at once, tier 3 holds it until the hit would end the duel. On defence: own pool always right; foreign pools right with the tier's accuracy, otherwise a random wrong counter from the phrase's pool. The Stakeholder never guesses and never attacks with a mic drop (every boss phrase is already worth a miss).

## Backgrounds

Four scenes, one per opponent role and one for the boss, all made with the `pixel-header` pipeline: Gemini prompt with a header as style reference, `snap.mjs` to the native grid, a per-scene loop script for two or three living details. Target 480 by 270 native pixels (16:9, no 5:2 crop this time; a 1376 px Gemini render snaps at its native 3 px grid to about 458 wide, and `sheet.mjs` pads it to 480 by 270 by repeating the plain edge columns and rows, which is why the edges must stay plain), the same 32-colour, 3 to 4 shades per material look as the headers. Scenes carry no people; the sprites are the people.

| Scene           | Used for           | Time and palette                                | Where the duellists stand                                              | Living details (looped)                                                                  |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Open-plan floor | Dev opponents      | Late afternoon, blue-grey with warm monitor light | Between two desk islands, monitors behind them as plain dark areas     | A monitor flicker, a CI light blinking green then red, a plant leaf in the AC draft      |
| Meeting room    | PM opponents       | Mid-morning, beige and whiteboard white         | In front of the whiteboard, table in the foreground framing the bottom | Projector fan dust in the beam, a coffee mug steaming, the whiteboard marker light       |
| Studio corner   | Designer opponents | Evening, warm wood and one cool desk lamp       | Beside a moodboard wall, printer on the left as a dark frame           | Lamp flicker, a sticky note lifting in a draft, a tablet screen pulsing on standby       |
| Corner office   | The Stakeholder    | Night, city lights behind glass, dark navy and amber | Desk between the duellists, window behind                          | City windows twinkling, phone screen glowing on the desk, rain streaks on the glass      |

Composition rule for all four: the middle band from y=60 to y=225 stays uncluttered where the sprites stand (x=60 to 170 for the left duellist, x=310 to 420 for the right). The top 40 px is a plain ceiling or sky and holds the two bars per duellist; the bottom 45 px a plain floor; the dialogue box overlays the floor. Dark framing shapes on the left and right edges, as in the headers.

Title and selection screens reuse the corner office at a darker exposure (no new scene).

Prompt: the `pixel-header` template as is, with two changes. The composition paragraph becomes `16:9 frame, about 480 pixels wide, with an empty plain middle band where two figures will stand later`, and `No people` stays. Living details are staged exactly as the skill says: in front of plain dark areas so the loop script can draw them cleanly.

Output per scene: `native.png`, `scene.webp` (lossless, 8 fps, 12 s loop) and `poster.png` for reduced motion, in `src/assets/lab/pushback/scenes/<slug>/`. Unlike the headers, the Gemini original is kept, in `src/assets/lab/pushback/sources/`, as the reference for later changes to the scene.

## Result card

After the boss, win or lose, a card over the darkened office with the run's numbers, and a `Copy` button that puts the same card on the clipboard as plain text (no analytics needed; this is the Wordle trick). The roster shows it again for a finished run.

```
Pushback · Dev
Stakeholder beaten · 10 duels · 5 flawless
Exchanges 31–9 · Mic drops 6
Missed counters: PM 3 · Dev 1 · Designer 5
sebald.me/lab/pushback
```

Everything on it comes from the stats in the save (see Tech). A lost boss fight says `Stakeholder took it offline` instead of `beaten`.

## Art and animation

Nothing is drawn by hand. Gemini generates every image (free tier), `snap.mjs` pulls it onto the pixel grid, scripts in the repo do the compositing, palette work and animation, and Claude Code reviews and repairs the pixels. Sprites stay at the pixel size Gemini draws them. The Dev sheet of 26 October (`sources/dev-sheet.jpg`, a 1376 by 768 render) snaps at a 6 px grid to figures about 74 px tall, 80 with the laptop raised; the height range for sheets is 70 to 85 px on a 72 by 112 cell, and roles must not differ in height for no reason (a sheet outside the range is rerolled, not scaled). Downscaling to 48 px lost the face and the headphones, so the stage is 480 by 270; a figure is then a bit under a third of the screen height, the classic adventure-game proportion. Same palette discipline as the headers (3 to 4 shades per material, dark outline on the silhouette only), same pixel size as the scene.

Why Gemini and not a sprite tool: the AI sprite services (PixelLab and similar) are paid, and the Claude Code pixel plugins only script Aseprite, which is paid or a self-compile; neither draws a better figure than a reference-steered Gemini prompt. Gemini holds a character steady inside one image, so every character is generated as a single model sheet, not frame by frame.

Pipeline per character:

1. Prompt Gemini for one model sheet: the figure six times in a row (idle, talk-open, taunt, flinch, win, lose), same character, same scale, front three-quarter view, plain dark background, a header attached as the style reference, the pixel-header template with `No people` removed and `Six copies of the same figure side by side, identical in size and colours, each in a different pose` added. Reroll until the six are the same person. PM, Designer and Stakeholder use the same prompt with the Dev sheet attached as a second reference, so scale and pixel size match.
2. `snap.mjs` at the sheet's detected grid (6 px on the Dev render), nothing forced; all six poses share one grid because they come from one image.
3. `sheet.mjs` cuts the strip into 72 by 112 cells by detecting the gaps, aligns each pose on its feet line, checks the figure height, keys out the background, locks the palette to the scene's 32 colours and writes the sheet plus a 4x contact print.
4. Claude Code reviews the contact print (it can view the PNG) against the checklist in the `pixel-sprite` skill: same height across poses, no stray single pixels, mouth and prop readable, palette within the scene. It fixes small faults in code (`sheet.mjs` has `setPx`-style helpers as in `loop.mjs`) and sends larger faults back to step 1 with a follow-up prompt in the same Gemini chat (`Keep this exactly, but …`).

Opponents are palette and prop swaps done in code, not new drawings: one model sheet per base role plus one for the Stakeholder, so four Gemini sheets in total. A swap is a colour lookup table in the character data plus an optional prop cell generated on its own (a 24 by 24 Gemini item, same prompt template) and pasted at a fixed anchor. Where a swap needs a head change (hood up, beard), that head is a separate 24 by 28 Gemini cell generated with the base sheet attached as reference. The Gemini originals are kept in the repo as `src/assets/lab/pushback/sources/<id>.jpg`; files under `src/assets` only ship when imported, so nothing excludes them from the build, they are simply never imported. They are the reference image for every later prompt, and a snapped PNG works worse as one.

Animation is code, not frames:

| Pose   | Source            | How it moves                                                                                      |
| ------ | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Idle   | Model sheet cell 1 | 1 px vertical bob every 600 ms, seeded like the header loops                                     |
| Talk   | Cells 1 and 2     | Alternate at 120 ms while the line types; a third mouth state is cell 1 with the mouth rows darkened by one shade |
| Taunt  | Cell 3            | Hold, plus 2 px step toward the opponent over 2 frames; on a mic drop the step is 4 px and the `Mic drop!` label appears |
| Flinch | Cell 4            | Hold 400 ms, 2 px step back, one frame of a one-shade-lighter overlay                             |
| Win    | Cell 5            | Hold, 1 px bob at double speed                                                                    |
| Lose   | Cell 6            | Hold, no bob                                                                                      |

If a sheet comes back with only four usable poses, the fallback is flinch for lose and taunt for win; the spec allows it and the data marks which cells exist. The right-hand duellist is the sheet mirrored; props with text are avoided in the prompts for that reason. The Stakeholder never leaves idle or talk during the barrage; the interrupt is the one time they flinch.

UI chrome is generated the same way at native size and cleaned by `sheet.mjs`: the dialogue box as a 9-slice, the buy-in bar (three or four segments, 10 by 6 px each) with the momentum bar below it (three 6 by 3 px steps), the silence bar as a 1 px line under the menu, and a pointing-hand cursor. A full momentum bar blinks at the idle bob's rhythm. Text is not drawn into the canvas: the dialogue line, the menus, the hand, the barks and the `Mic drop!` label are DOM in the site's Fira Code, laid over the stage like the hotspot remarks under the note headers (`font-mono`, small, with the same one-pixel text shadow). The overlay text never goes below 12 px, so at 1x on a phone the dialogue box and menu grow past the drawn chrome; the 70-character limit in Lines fits two rows at every integer scale from 2x up.

Rendering: scene, sprites and chrome are composited into a 480 by 270 canvas with `imageSmoothingEnabled = false`, scaled to the largest integer factor that fits the viewport (2x on laptops, 3x on 1080p, 4x on 1440p, 1x on phones in landscape with a rotate hint in portrait). The DOM overlay is positioned in the same scaled frame. `loop.mjs` gets a `scene-1x.webp` export so scenes and sprites share one pixel grid.

## Tech

The game is a client component on a new route in the existing Next.js app; no new framework, no game engine. The repo's stack (Next 16, React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind 4, oxlint, Node test runner) covers everything, and the state machine is plain enough to unit-test without a browser.

Data shapes (the spec's tables map 1:1 onto these):

```typescript
type Role = 'pm' | 'dev' | 'designer';

type Pair = { id: string; role: Role; phrase: string; counter: string };
type BossPair = { id: string; phrase: string; answeredBy: Pair['id'] };

type Tier = 1 | 2 | 3 | 'boss';

type Opponent = {
  id: string; name: string; role: Role | 'boss';
  tier: Tier; throws: (Pair | BossPair)['id'][];
  accuracy: number;                 // on foreign pools; own pool is always 1
  menu: number;                     // counter options on defence
  decoys: 'any' | 'two' | 'same' | 'all';
  silence: number | null;           // seconds, null = off
  sheet: { base: Role | 'boss'; head?: string; prop?: string; accessory?: string };
  palette?: Record<string, string>; scene: 'floor' | 'meeting' | 'studio' | 'office';
  intro: string;
};

type Rules = {
  buyIn: number;                    // 3, boss duel 4
  momentum: number;                 // 3
  micDrop: { worth: number; menu: number; silence: number };  // 2, 8, 0.5
  hand: number;                     // 4
  bossMisses: number;               // 3
};

type RunStats = {
  exchangesWon: number; exchangesLost: number; micDrops: number;
  missesByPool: Record<Role, number>;
};

type Save = {
  version: 1;
  silence: boolean;                 // the silence bar switch on the title screen
  runs: Partial<Record<Role, {
    beaten: Record<Opponent['id'], { flawless: boolean }>;
    finished: boolean; stats: RunStats;
  }>>;
};
```

Duel state machine (one reducer, screens derived from `phase`):

1. `intro`: opponent bark, then `attack` with the opponent holding the initiative (the challenger always opens).
2. `attack`: attacker's line types out. Player attacker picks from the hand of four and, with full momentum, toggles the mic drop first; AI picks per Opponents.
3. `defend`: counter menu (player, with the silence bar sending `tick` actions and a `timeout` that plays the fallback) or AI pick. Resolves to `resolve`.
4. `resolve`: taunt/flinch poses, buy-in segment fills, momentum updates; on a player miss the right counter is shown for one beat. Initiative to the winner. Full buy-in → `end`, else `attack`.
5. `end`: win/lose poses and barks (the boss's loss bark keyed to the pool of the last missed counter), save written with the stats, then back to the roster or, after the boss, the result card and the credits line.

The boss duel runs the same reducer with `buyIn: 4`, the initiative pinned to the boss, a miss counter that ends the duel at three, and the interrupt as the one `attack` phase the player gets, entered from `resolve` when momentum is full.

Rendering loop: a single `requestAnimationFrame` loop at 8 fps steps (125 ms, matching the scene loops) draws scene frame, left sprite, right sprite and chrome into the 480 by 270 canvas. Dialogue and menus are real DOM (text and buttons overlaid on the canvas, styled to match) so they are accessible and tappable; the canvas is decorative and gets `aria-hidden`, the current line is also written to a visually hidden live region, and the bars have text equivalents there (`Buy-in 2 of 3, momentum full`).

Input: arrow keys or W/S to move in menus, Enter or Space to confirm and to skip typing, M to toggle the mic drop when available, Escape to pause. Touch: tap a menu item, tap the stage to skip typing, tap the momentum bar to toggle the mic drop. The whole thing works with a screen reader through the DOM menus and the live region, with the silence bar off.

Reduced motion: scenes show the poster, idle bob and talk cycle are off, typing is instant, the silence bar defaults to off. Same rule as the headers.

Tests: `duel.ts` and `ai.ts` under the Node test runner (buy-in and initiative, momentum gains and resets, mic drop worth and cost, the hand never repeats a phrase within a duel, decoy rules and menu size per tier, own-pool answers are always right, the silence timeout plays the fallback as a miss, the boss barrage ends on the sixth phrase under every outcome, the interrupt fires only at full momentum, boss mapping completeness: every boss phrase has exactly one answering pair that exists; every opponent's `throws` ids exist; every line under 70 characters; the result card text matches the stats). `sheets.test.ts` validates the committed sheets. One Playwright VRT screenshot of the title screen and one of a frozen duel frame, added to the existing visual suite (ask before running VRT, per `AGENTS.md`).

## Build plan

Seven work packages, each a mergeable PR with its own tests, ordered so the game is playable with placeholder art from package 3 onward. Art lands last and in parallel, because it is the part most likely to be interrupted.

- [ ] **1. Data and rules** (`lines.ts`, `characters.ts`, `duel.ts`, `ai.ts`, tests). Done when a scripted duel and a scripted boss barrage run headless in a test, the boss mapping check passes, and hands, AI choices and decoy menus are deterministic under a seed.
- [ ] **2. Save and routes** (`save.ts`, `/lab/pushback` page and client wrapper, title with the silence switch, role-select and roster screens as DOM, result card text). Done when a role can be picked, a run persists across reloads and the roster reflects `beaten` and the stars.

Playable grey box** (canvas stage, dialogue box, hand and counter menus, the two bars and the silence bar as flat rectangles, typing, keyboard and touch input, placeholder rectangles for sprites and a flat background). ## After v1

Only if v1 feels thin after a few weeks of play; both are small once the rules are in data.

- **Role passives**, one each, matching the select-screen blurbs: PM redraws the hand once per duel, Dev pauses the silence bar once per duel (`Let me check`), Designer gets one miss forgiven per duel (`It's a draft`). Gives the three roles different feels and an honest reason to finish all three.
- **Daily seed**: hands and decoys seeded from the date, so the same run exists for everyone that day and the result cards compare.
